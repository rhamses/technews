As someone who joined ClickHouse with no prior experience in marketing analytics and finding myself contributing content on a regular basis in the form of blogs, I had long assumed Google Analytics (GA4) provided a fast and seamless way to measure a website. So when tasked with reporting on the success of our content strategy, with the aim of ensuring that we were producing content relevant to you, our users, GA4 seemed like the obvious place to start.</p>\n<p>With a naive enthusiasm, I formulated a set of questions that I assumed would be trivial for us to answer in GA4, such as “What does the distribution of views per blog look like from the day they are published?” The reaction of Adrian, our head of Marketing Operations, “politely” communicated this would take some time to formulate. Realizing we needed to report this on a monthly basis and seeing him busy with other more important tasks, I offered my own time to assist.</p>\n<p>Reporting on blog performance quickly became the day of the month I most dreaded. While Google Analytics has its strengths, not least its ease of integration and use, it became apparent it was limited in a number of critical ways: data retention, sampling, performance, and flexibility. Rather than toil away in GA4 and dread the 2nd Monday of each month, we undertook a project to move all Google Analytics data to ClickHouse, with the aim of delivering flexible, fast analytics with infinite retention.</p>\n<p>In this blog post, we explain our architecture, which hopefully fellow sufferers can use to build their own super-charged Google Analytics using only ClickHouse and a few lines of SQL. Most importantly, this can all be achieved for less than $20 a month using ClickHouse Cloud!</p>\n<h2 id=\"challenges-with-ga4\"></h2>\n<p><strong>Flexibility.</strong> Google Analytics' strength is its ease of integration to a site and its simple query interface. This simplicity comes at a cost, principally with flexibility. The interface, while easy to use, is restrictive and limits the user's ability to answer more complex questions such as \"What is the distribution of views for blogs by the day of release?\" Many of our questions also required external datasets, such as reading time and blog themes. While we were often able to overcome these challenges by either exporting data and using <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/blog/extracting-converting-querying-local-files-with-sql-clickhouse-local\">clickhouse local</a> to query the files or using GA4's <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/3191589?hl=en#zippy=%2Cin-this-article\">import data feature</a>, the process was sluggish and time-consuming. As someone who considers themselves semi-technical, I longed for the flexibility of SQL.</p>\n<p><strong>Performance.</strong> Clickhouse.com is highly trafficked  - in the 10s of thousands per day. While this might seem high, this volume of data is, in reality, very small for the sizes we're used to at ClickHouse. Despite this, the GA4 interface was always sluggish, with queries taking a long time to load. This made iterating on queries an extremely frustrating experience. And, as a company used to performing real-time analytics on data with lightning-fast response times, this performance was, at best frustrating and, at worst, led to us shy away from asking new  questions about our data.</p>\n<p><strong>Sampling &amp; ad-hoc queries.</strong> Given the comparatively low volumes of data, it was surprising that queries in Google Analytics often reported that the data was being sampled. For us, this manifested itself when issuing ad-hoc queries (reports seemed more reliable) that used a large number of dimensions or spanned a wide time period. GA4 provides means to <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/13331292?sjid=3850789083668214302-EU\">work around this</a>, including upgrading to Google Analytics 360 (for $150,000 per year!) or simply waiting a long time for results. Neither of these was acceptable to us, and the frustration of having unreliable results was particularly frustrating to us, as ClickHouse users who are used to fast, precise responses.</p>\n<p><span><img alt=\"ga_sampling.png\" src=\"https://clickhouse.com/_next/image?url=%2Fuploads%2Fga_sampling_7990a06a46.png&w=2048&q=75\"></span></p>\n<p><strong>Data-retention.</strong> By default, GA4 retains data for two months. This can be <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/7667196?hl=en\">increased to 14 months</a>, with 360 (again, for $150,000 a year) required for longer periods. While we currently don’t have years of data, we wanted to be able to identify patterns over time, e.g. seasonal trends, using more than 2 data points.</p>\n<p><span><img alt=\"ga_data_retention.png\" src=\"https://clickhouse.com/_next/image?url=%2Fuploads%2Fga_data_retention_cccdce7754.png&w=2048&q=75\"></span></p>\n<h2 id=\"why-clickhouse-for-google-analytics-data\"></h2>\n<p>While ClickHouse was the obvious choice for us, as a dogfooding exercise, it is also the de facto database used for web analytics - the word “Click” comes from Click analytics, the original Google Analytics-like use case for which the database was developed.</p>\n<p>As a real-time data warehouse supporting SQL, ClickHouse provides the required query flexibility we need. Almost all of our queries could be easily represented as SQL. ClickHouse dictionaries also provided the perfect solution to integrate our external data sources, such as blog themes and reading times.</p>\n<p>Most of these queries comprise aggregations for which ClickHouse is optimized as a column-oriented database, capable of delivering sub-second response times on hundreds of billions of rows <em>without</em> sampling - well beyond the scale we saw from GA4.</p>\n<p>ClickHouse supports a wide range of integrations that make the generation of reports simpler. As well as <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/blog/clickhouse-cloud-compatible-with-mysql\">supporting the MySQL interface</a>, allowing the use of tools such as <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/docs/en/integrations/looker\">Looker</a>, <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/docs/en/integrations/tableau-online\">Tableau</a>, and <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/docs/en/integrations/quicksight\">QuickSight</a>, native support for tools such as Superset and Grafana offered an open-source BI experience. Finally, we believed the raw data would be compressed well by ClickHouse and potentially kept indefinitely at a low cost - especially since ClickCloud Cloud uses object storage.</p>\n<h2 id=\"internal-data-warehouse\"></h2>\n<p>At this point, it became obvious that we could solve more than simply blog reporting. Our marketing team was suffering from the same challenges identified above when reporting on wider site metrics.</p>\n<p>As a company that prides itself on data-driven decision-making, we already have a <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/blog/building-a-data-warehouse-with-clickhouse\">dedicated team responsible for our internal data warehouse</a>. If we could identify a simple way of making the data available and provide most of the required queries, we could utilize their existing techniques for loading, managing, and visualizing data.</p>\n<blockquote>\n<p>While ClickHouse would be an ideal data store for web analytics data, we still wanted to retain the data collection capabilities of GA4 and Google Tag Manager. We had little interest in writing our own Javascript library responsible for session tracking and data collection. Ideally, we could simply ensure the data was exported to ClickHouse at a reasonable frequency.</p>\n</blockquote>\n<h2 id=\"getting-data-out-of-ga4\"></h2>\n<p>Believing it was unlikely that the pains we were experiencing above were unique, we explored ways to export data from Google Analytics. Google offers a <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://analyticscanvas.com/4-ways-to-export-ga4-data/\">number of ways to achieve this</a>, most of which come with some limitations. The best solution appeared to be exporting the data to BigQuery. This had a number of advantages over other solutions, such as the Data API, including:</p>\n<ul>\n<li>This would export the raw data with no sampling.</li>\n<li>Google allows up to <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/9823238?sjid=16140205916940793336-EU#limits&zippy=%2Cin-this-article\">1m events per day</a> to be exported as a <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/9823238?sjid=16140205916940793336-EU#step3&zippy=%2Cin-this-article\">batch to a daily table</a>. This was sufficient for our needs and below our current thresholds. We may need to ask Google to increase this in the future.</li>\n<li>Data can be exported in a <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/9358801?sjid=16140205916940793336-EU#streaming\">streaming mode to an intraday table</a> as well as a daily export. The intraday “live” table typically lags real-time by a few minutes. Most importantly, this export has no limits! It does, however, not contain all of the same events (although it conforms to the same schema) - preventing some queries from being run on real-time data. This interestingly opened the possibility of real-time dashboards!</li>\n</ul>\n<blockquote>\n<p>Streaming exports incur around $0.05 per gigabyte of data. 1 gigabyte equates to approximately 600,000 Google Analytics events. This should be insignificant for more users.</p>\n</blockquote>\n<p>Configuration of this connection is simple and <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://support.google.com/analytics/answer/9823238#zippy=%2Cin-this-article\">well documented</a>, provided you have BigQuery enabled for your Google Cloud account.</p>\n<p><span><img alt=\"big_query_ga.png\" src=\"https://clickhouse.com/_next/image?url=%2Fuploads%2Fbig_query_ga_e21dd2aa51.png&w=2048&q=75\"></span></p>\n<p>Maybe the obvious question then becomes, “Why not just use BigQuery for your analytics?”</p>\n<p><strong>Cost and performance</strong>. We wanted to run queries regularly with real-time dashboards, especially with access to live data. While BigQuery is great for ad-hoc analytics on complex queries, it charges for data scanned, making costs unpredictable. Conversely, ClickHouse Cloud serves these queries at a fixed cost with a small cluster (e.g. Development Tier service at &lt; $200 per month). Furthermore, BigQuery typically incurs a minimum latency on queries. We knew that ClickHouse would deliver millisecond response times and is better suited to the flat schema (only two tables) and aggregation-heavy queries. Further details on the differences between BigQuery and ClickHouse can be found <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/blog/clickhouse-bigquery-migrating-data-for-realtime-queries\">here</a>.</p>\n<h2 id=\"bigquery-to-clickhouse\"></h2>\n<p>Details on how data can be migrated between BigQuery and ClickHouse can be found in our documentation <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/docs/en/migrations/bigquery\">here</a>. In summary, we rely on two scheduled queries to export data to a GCS bucket in Parquet: one for the daily table (format <code>events_YYYYMMDD</code>) and one for the live intraday table (format <code>events_intraday_YYYYMMDD</code>). Parquet is our preferred export format due to its good compression, structured schema, and excellent support in <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/blog/clickhouse-release-23-08\">ClickHouse for fast reading</a>. Users can then use either scheduled <code>INSERT INTO SELECT</code> queries, using cron services and the <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/docs/en/sql-reference/table-functions/gcs\">gcs table function</a>, or the recently released <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/docs/en/engines/table-engines/integrations/s3queue\">S3Queue</a> to import this data into ClickHouse.</p>\n<p><span><img alt=\"arch_bigquery_to_clickhouse.png\" src=\"https://clickhouse.com/_next/image?url=%2Fuploads%2Farch_bigquery_to_clickhouse_35fa7faa16.png&w=2048&q=75\"></span></p>\n<p>We provide more detail on this architecture below.</p>\n<h3 id=\"bigquery-export\"></h3>\n<p>To export data from BigQuery, we rely on s<a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://cloud.google.com/bigquery/docs/scheduling-queries\">cheduled queries</a> and their ability to <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://cloud.google.com/bigquery/docs/exporting-data\">export to GCS</a>.</p>\n<p>We found that the daily table would be created for the previous day at around 4 pm GMT.
