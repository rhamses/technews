contentHtml: "<div><h2 id=\"introduction\"></h2>\n<p>Almost one year ago, we blogged about the<a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/blog/the-state-of-sql-based-observability\"> state of SQL-based observability</a>, exploring two parallel backgrounds of two established paradigms: SQL and observability. We explained how they had collided and together created a new array of opportunities in the field of observability while attempting to answer \"When is SQL-based observability applicable to my use case?\" This blog post garnered significant attention and has proved useful in discussing ClickHouse and its role within the observability space to new users.</p>\n<p>Although the general themes of the original blog post remain true, a year is a long time in ClickHouse development! As well as multiple new features which we believe will further move ClickHouse to the position as the de-facto database for observability data, the eco-system surrounding ClickHouse has matured, simplifying the adoption for new users.</p>\n<p>In this post, we'll explore some of these new features as well as give a glimpse into some experimental work which we hope will form the basis for ClickHouse's use in the less explored pillar of metrics within observability.</p>\n<h2 id=\"the-state-of-sql-based-observability-in-2023\"></h2>\n<p>Our original post proposed that observability is just another data problem, to which SQL and <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/engineering-resources/oltp-vs-olap\">OLAP based systems</a>, such as ClickHouse, are well suited to addressing. We explored the history of centralized logging, and how solutions such as Splunk and the ELK stack had emerged from the both syslog and NoSQL era. Despite the popularization of the former, SQL’s unique strengths have sustained its relevance, making it the<a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://survey.stackoverflow.co/2023/#most-popular-technologies-language-prof\"> third most widely adopted language</a> for structured data management, even amidst the NoSQL boom.</p>\n<p>By rethinking observability as a data problem, we can apply OLAP principles and SQL to the storage layer, avoiding the need for cost-saving measures like <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/resources/engineering/managing-petabyte-scale-logs-without-sampling\">aggressive log sampling</a> that ultimately undermine visibility. This approach brings key benefits: efficient compression reduces storage demands, accelerated data ingestion and retrieval, and limitless storage enabled by the use of object storage. The versatility of SQL-based observability is amplified by interoperability, with ClickHouse, for instance, supporting a broad range of data formats and integration engines, simplifying its inclusion in diverse observability pipelines. SQL’s extensive functions offer unmatched analytical expressivity, and together, these features <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/blog/breaking-free-from-rising-observability-costs-with-open-cost-efficient-architectures\">lower the total cost of ownership (TCO) of observability data</a>.</p>\n<p><span><img alt=\"img08_9763861f2e\" src=\"https://clickhouse.com/_next/image?url=%2Fuploads%2Fimg08_9763861f2e.png&w=2048&q=75\"></span></p>\n<p>An important enabler of this shift has been OpenTelemetry, which standardized data collection across platforms and has turned data collection from a differentiator into a commodity. A year on we can confidently say <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/engineering-resources/opentelemetry-otel\">OTel</a> is well on its way to winning the \"collector wars.\"</p>\n<p>This industry-wide adoption has <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/blog/breaking-free-from-rising-observability-costs-with-open-cost-efficient-architectures\">reduced vendor lock-in</a> and made it easier to integrate observability data with SQL-based storage solutions.</p>\n<p><span><img alt=\"img09_4566662115\" src=\"https://clickhouse.com/_next/image?url=%2Fuploads%2Fimg09_4566662115.png&w=2048&q=75\"></span></p>\n<p>We concluded that the resulting SQL-based observability stack is simple and not opinionated, leaving many options for the users to personalize, adapt, and integrate within an existing IT environment.</p>\n<p>With this in mind, and in the interest of ensuring our users are successful, we provided a simple checklist to help users determine if SQL-based observability was them. Specifically,</p>\n<p>SQL-based observability is for you if:</p>\n<ul>\n<li>You or your team(s) are familiar with SQL (or want to learn it)</li>\n<li>You prefer adhering to open standards like OpenTelemetry to avoid lock-in and achieve extensibility.</li>\n<li>You are willing to run an ecosystem fueled by open-source innovation from collection to storage and visualization.</li>\n<li>You envision some growth to medium or large volumes of observability data under management (or even very large volumes)</li>\n<li>You want to be in control of the TCO (total cost of ownership) and <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/blog/breaking-free-from-rising-observability-costs-with-open-cost-efficient-architectures\">break free from rising observability costs</a> common with legacy, seat-based platforms.</li>\n<li>You can’t or don’t want to get stuck with small data retention periods for your observability data just to manage the costs.</li>\n</ul>\n<p>SQL-based observability may not be for you if:</p>\n<ul>\n<li>Learning (or generating!) SQL is not appealing to you or your team(s).</li>\n<li>You are looking for a packaged, end-to-end observability experience.</li>\n<li>Your observability data volumes are too small to make any significant difference (e.g. &lt;150 GiB) and are not forecasted to grow.</li>\n<li>Your use case is metrics-heavy and needs PromQL. In that case, you can still use ClickHouse for logs and tracing beside Prometheus for metrics, unifying it at the presentation layer with Grafana.</li>\n<li>You prefer to wait for the ecosystem to mature more and SQL-based observability to get more turnkey.</li>\n</ul>\n<p><strong>The question, as 2024 draws to a close, is: has this changed given the developments in ClickHouse?</strong></p>\n<h2 id=\"json-support-a-game-changer-for-observability\"></h2>\n<p>Before discussing the recent developments in JSON support, it's important to outline both what we mean by JSON support, why it's important for observability, and the challenges our users currently face.</p>\n<h3 id=\"what-do-we-mean-by-json-support\"></h3>\n<p>ClickHouse has long supported JSON as both an input and output format, which paved the way for its early integration into observability tools. JSON data can be sent over ClickHouse’s native and HTTP interfaces, with a variety of <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/docs/en/interfaces/formats\">output formats to fit specific needs</a>. This flexibility made it straightforward to integrate with tools like OpenTelemetry and Grafana, enabling smooth data ingestion and visualization. Furthermore, it allowed users to <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/blog/building-single-page-applications-with-clickhouse-and-http\">build custom interfaces easily</a>, making ClickHouse adaptable to many observability use cases.</p>\n<p><span><img alt=\"Blog_SQLObservabilityDiagrams_202411_V1.0-01.png\" src=\"https://clickhouse.com/_next/image?url=%2Fuploads%2FBlog_SQL_Observability_Diagrams_202411_V1_0_01_ae31271ea9.png&w=2048&q=75\"></span></p>\n<p>However, JSON integration differs from <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/engineering-resources/json-database\">full support for JSON as a column type</a> — an increasingly important feature in observability. By this, we mean the ability to designate a column as JSON, which can store nested dynamic structures whose values have potentially different data types (possibly incompatible and unknown beforehand) for the same paths.</p>\n<p><span><img alt=\"Blog_SQLObservabilityDiagrams_202411_V1.0-02.png\" src=\"https://clickhouse.com/_next/image?url=%2Fuploads%2FBlog_SQL_Observability_Diagrams_202411_V1_0_02_e85e272b8e.png&w=2048&q=75\"></span></p>\n<h3 id=\"why-is-json-support-important\"></h3>\n<p>Users deploying observability solutions need the ability to just \"send events\" without having to worry about schema refinement or optimizations. Even when time can be spent defining schemas, centralized observability systems commonly aggregate data from diverse sources, each with unique or evolving data structures. This data might come from different applications, teams, or even entirely separate organizations, especially within SaaS solutions. In these scenarios, a universal naming convention or schema for all data is challenging to achieve.</p>\n<p>While structured logging and OpenTelemetry schema standards and<a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://opentelemetry.io/docs/concepts/semantic-conventions/\"> semantic conventions</a> help address this issue, users still need to capture arbitrary tags and fields. These fields can vary significantly in quantity, type, and level of nesting depending on the source. To address this, a column dedicated to \"unstructured JSON\" data is invaluable, enabling flexible storage of dynamic attributes such as Kubernetes labels or custom application-specific metadata.</p>\n<p>Ideally, ClickHouse would support a JSON column type, enabling users to send semi-structured data directly. This approach would offer the same capabilities, compression, and performance as traditional data types while reducing the effort required for schema management and definition.</p>\n<h3 id=\"challenges-of-current-approaches\"></h3>\n<p>Currently, the OTel exporter for ClickHouse provides default schemas for logs, traces, and metrics. <code>ScopeAttributes.</code> These columns in OpenTelemetry capture metadata at different levels: <code>LogAttributes</code> hold details specific to individual log events, <code>ResourceAttributes</code> contain information about the source generating the data, and <code>ScopeAttributes</code> store context about the application code or instrumentation. Together, these attributes provide essential context for filtering and grouping. Importantly, they are entirely use-case dependent and can vary significantly based on the source and application, as well as potentially changing over time.</p>\n<p>While the Map type allowed users to store these fields by supporting dynamic addition of keys, it incurs some significant disadvantages:</p>\n<ul>\n<li><strong>Loss of type precision</strong> - Field name and values in this representation are held as Strings, with the Map only supporting a single type as the value (users can now use<a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/docs/en/sql-reference/data-types/variant\"> Variant</a> as the value to the Map). The String type is applied here as the \"unified\" type. This meant more complex comparisons on values that were numeric required a query time cast. This is both more verbose and inefficient, impacting query latency and memory overhead of a query.</li>\n<li><strong>Single-column implementation with linear complexity</strong> - All of the nested JSON paths are held in a single-column value. When accessing a specific known key in the Map, ClickHouse is required to<a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/docs/knowledgebase/improve-map-performance\"> load all values in the Map and linearly scan</a>. This incurs a significant IO overhead, which is effectively wasted when only a single path is being read, making queries on maps with large numbers of keys inefficient and slower than users expect. To address this, users would extract values from the Map using<a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/docs/en/observability/schema-design#materialized-columns\"> materialized columns at insert time</a><strong>.</strong></li>\n<li><strong>Obfuscation of available keys</strong> - By storing the JSON paths as map keys, the user is unable to identify the available paths for querying without a query. While<a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/docs/en/observability/schema-design#using-maps\"> possible, this requires an expensive linear scan</a> in order to identify all available keys. While this can be addressed with materialized views to compute this set at insert time, it adds additional complexity and requires UI tooling to integrate with the output in order to provide useful query guidance features.</li>\n<li><strong>Lack of support for nested structures</strong> - Using a String as the Map value type limits it to one level only. Other types in ClickHouse, such as <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/docs/en/sql-reference/data-types/tuple\">Tuple</a> and <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/docs/en/sql-reference/data-types/nested-data-structures/nested\">Nested</a>, supported nested structures but these required specification of the structure in advance of receiving the data.</li>\n</ul>\n<p>The new JSON type addresses all of these challenges, providing an efficient means of supporting dynamically nested structures while retaining precision and the performance associated with statically typed columns.</p>\n<h3 id=\"efficient-json-support\"></h3>\n<p>When our original blog post was published, ClickHouse’s JSON support was in its infancy. The feature had existed as experimental for some time but had significant challenges. Most of these arose from both its inability to handle high numbers of JSON paths efficiently but also its attempt to handle different types for the same path by dynamically unifying their types. This turned out to be a fundamental design flaw, requiring us to rewrite the feature from the ground up. <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/blog/clickhouse-release-24-08\">In 24.8, we announced</a> that this rewrite was largely complete and was on the path to being beta with a high degree of confidence its implementation was as good as theoretically possible.</p>\n<p><a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/blog/a-new-powerful-json-data-type-for-clickhouse\">As we detailed in a recent blog</a>, this <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/docs/en/sql-reference/data-types/newjson\">new JSON implementation</a> in ClickHouse is purpose-built to address the unique challenges of handling JSON in a columnar database while avoiding the pitfalls of type unification or enforcement, which can distort data or make querying more challenging.</p>\n<p>Many <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://www.elastic.co/guide/en/elasticsearch/reference/current/dynamic-mapping.html\">other data stores</a> aim to address these challenges by inferring the type from the first event in which a path is encountered - an approach our original JSON implementation adopted. Future events with different types for a path are either coerced or rejected. This is sub-optimal for observability as rarely events can be guaranteed to be clean and consistent. The result is either poor query performance or a reduced set of operators exposed to the user unless query-time casting is performed.</p>\n<p>As shown below, ClickHouse stores the values of each unique JSON path in a true columnar format, even supporting cases where paths have different types. This is accomplished by creating a separate sub-column for each unique path and type pair.</p>\n<p><span><img alt=\"json_column_per_type.png\" src=\"https://clickhouse.com/_next/image?url=%2Fuploads%2Fjson_column_per_type_ec55df6761.png&w=2048&q=75\"></span></p>\n<p>For example, when two JSON paths are inserted with differing types, ClickHouse stores the values of each <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/blog/a-new-powerful-json-data-type-for-clickhouse#storage-extension-for-dynamically-changing-data\">concrete type in distinct sub-columns</a>. These sub-columns can be accessed independently, minimizing unnecessary I/O. Note that when querying a column with multiple types, its values are still returned as a single columnar response.</p>\n<p>Additionally, by <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/blog/a-new-powerful-json-data-type-for-clickhouse#dense-data-storage\">leveraging offsets</a>, ClickHouse ensures that these sub-columns remain dense, with no default values stored for absent JSON paths. This approach maximizes compression and further reduces I/O.</p>\n<p><span><img alt=\"json_offsets_column.png\" src=\"https://clickhouse.com/_next/image?url=%2Fuploads%2Fjson_overflow_column_8c25f051d9.png&w=2048&q=75\"></span></p>\n<p>By capturing each unique JSON path as a distinct sub-column, ClickHouse can also efficiently provide a list of available paths, enabling UIs to leverage features like auto-complete.</p>\n<p>Furthermore, the type does not suffer from sub-column explosion issues resulting from a higher number of unique JSON paths. Note that this doesn’t prevent storage of these paths; rather, it simply stores new paths in a single shared data column if limits are exceeded (with statistics to accelerate queries).</p>\n<p><span><img alt=\"json_overflow.png\" src=\"https://clickhouse.com/_next/image?url=%2Fuploads%2Fjson_overflow_669080ab2f.png&w=2048&q=75\"></span></p>\n<p>With this enhanced JSON data type, users benefit from scalable, high-performance analytics on complex, nested JSON structures - making ClickHouse’s JSON support as efficient and fast as any classic data type in its system.</p>\n<p>For users curious about the implementation of this new column type, we recommend reading our<a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/blog/a-new-powerful-json-data-type-for-clickhouse\"> detailed blog post here</a>.</p>\n<h3 id=\"json-for-otel\"></h3>\n<p>We expect this feature to be exposed in the OpenTelemetry schema for ClickHouse. While ClickHouse is fast for linear scans, on larger deployments this becomes unrealistic if users expect sub second response times. Typically filtered columns should always be<a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/docs/en/optimize/sparse-primary-indexes\"> located in the primary key, sensibly ordered to align with the drill down behavior</a> i.e. the first and most common filtered column should be first. This aligns with our earlier efforts to produce a better getting-started schema for users.</p>\n<p>Additionally, it has become clear that materialized views are essential to meeting performance expectations and transforming OTel data to fit users' access patterns. We increasingly see users sending their data to a Null table engine, with a materialized view executing a query on the inserted rows. The results of this view are sent to target tables, which are responsible for serving user queries.</p>\n<p><span><img alt=\"materialized_views.png\" src=\"https://clickhouse.com/_next/image?url=%2Fuploads%2Fmaterialized_views_361c981679.png&w=2048&q=75\"></span></p>\n<p>This has a few clear benefits:</p>\n<ul>\n<li>\n<p><strong>Transformation</strong> - allowing users to modify the structure of data prior to storage. While the OTel schema is broadly applicable, users often need to extract fields from unstructured data e.g. the <code>Body</code>, for efficient querying or use in the primary key. While transformations such as parsing strings or processing JSON can be done in the OTel collector, this work is more efficiently performed in ClickHouse where it consumes less resources and is faster. For an example, see the <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/docs/en/observability/schema-design#extracting-structure-with-sql\">Extracting structure with SQL</a> section of the schema design guide.</p>\n</li>\n<li>\n<p><strong>Alternative ordering keys</strong> - Often, users' access patterns are too diverse to align with a single primary key. In this case, we see users either exploiting<a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/docs/en/observability/schema-design#using-projections\"> projections</a> or using a materialized view that replicates subsets of the data to tables with a different ordering key. These separate tables then serve sub-teams or specific dashboards with known workflows. Alternatively, we find users using these sub tables to act as fast lookup tables for values then used to filter on the main table. For an example, see the <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/docs/en/observability/schema-design#using-materialized-views-incremental--for-fast-lookups\">Using Materialized views (incremental) for fast lookups</a> section of the schema design guide.</p>\n</li>\n<li>\n<p><strong>Filtering subsets</strong> - Materialized views are increasingly used to filter subsets, sending these to tables on which alerts are generated. This allows the cost of these queries to be shifted from query to insert time.</p>\n</li>\n<li>\n<p><strong>Precomputing aggregations</strong> - Similar to filtering, precomputing aggregations queries in materialized views, such as error counts over time, allows the computational overhead to be shifted from insert to query time. For an example, see the <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/docs/en/observability/schema-design#using-materialized-views-incremental-for-aggregations\">Using Materialized views (incremental) for aggregations</a> section of the schema design guide.</p>\n</li>\n</ul>\n<p>Finally, we’ve found <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/docs/en/observability/schema-design#using-aliases\">users exploiting Column Alias’ in ClickHouse</a> - especially in cases where the map columns identified earlier have many keys. This simplifies querying for users and avoids the need for users to remember available keys. While we expect this to be less of an issue with the development of JSON support, we still expect these to be relevant. Aliases are, as a result, also <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/docs/en/integrations/grafana/config#column-aliases\">supported in the ClickHouse plugin for Grafana</a> for users who prefer to manage these through the interface layer.</p>\n<blockquote>\n<p>We have reflected these lessons in our <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/docs/en/observability\">Observability guide</a> published earlier this year.</p>\n</blockquote>\n<h3 id=\"one-size-doesn-t-fit-all-not-always-otel\"></h3>\n<p>While OpenTelemetry brings a standardized, flexible, and open approach to telemetry data, using a powerful <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/engineering-resources/what-is-real-time-analytics\">real-time analytics</a> database such as ClickHouse enables it to store telemetry data beyond the OTel paradigm efficiently.
