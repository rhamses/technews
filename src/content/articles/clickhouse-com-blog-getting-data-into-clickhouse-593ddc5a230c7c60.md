However, to give users some context for future ClickHouse queries and a basic understanding of the data, we briefly explain the core concepts. Understanding forex trading is not mandatory to be able to follow the technical aspects in this post, but will help explain the motivations behind the queries.</p>\n<p>Forex trading is the trading of currencies from different countries against each other; for example, the US Dollar against the Euro. Any currency trade occurs between a pair of currencies. The purchasing of a currency pair involves two parties, such as a trader and a broker. The trader can either buy a <strong>base</strong> currency with a <strong>quote</strong> currency from the broker or sell a <strong>base</strong> currency and receive the <strong>quote</strong> in return. This pair is quoted in the format X/Y, where X is the base and Y is the quote. For example, the sample data below shows the bid and quote prices for the pair EUR/USD (a <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://www.vantagemarkets.com/education/types-of-forex-currency-pairs-majors-v-minors-v-exotic-v-currency-crosses/\">major</a> pair) in 2022.</p>\n<p>Given the above, the <strong>ask quote</strong> (or ask price) represents the price the broker is willing to accept in quote currency for every unit of the base currency they are selling, i.e., it is what they are asking for. This is what the trader can open a BUY position at.</p>\n<p>The <strong>bid quote</strong> (or bid/offer price) is what the broker is willing to buy or BID in quote currency for the base currency. The trader opens a SELL (short) position at this price.</p>\n<p>The ask quote will always be higher than the bid quote. The trader will always pay more for the pair than what they can get back. The difference between the bid and ask is the spread and is effectively the margin for the broker and how institutions make money.</p>\n<p>For example, consider the following quote for the EUR/USD pair from 02/09/2022 from <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://www.forex.com/en/forex-trading/eur-usd/\">forex.com</a>.</p>\n<p><span><img alt=\"eur_usd.png\" src=\"https://clickhouse.com/_next/image?url=%2Fuploads%2Feur_usd_c629e859e3.png&w=2048&q=75\"></span></p>\n<h2 id=\"pips-and-ticks\"></h2>\n<p>The minimum unit of measurement when trading a currency pair is the Point in Price or PiP. This is typically the <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://mytradingskills.com/what-is-tick-size\">4th decimal place</a> for most major currency pairs or, in some cases, the 2nd, e.g., for pairs involving the Japanese yen. A currency pair’s prices are, in turn, usually measured one decimal place higher. Note that spreads are also measured in pips.</p>\n<p><a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://www.valutrades.com/en/blog/pips-points-and-ticks-whats-the-difference\">Ticks record</a> when the price of a stock or commodity changes by a predetermined amount or fractional change, i.e., a tick occurs when the price moves up or down by a specific amount or fractional change. In forex, a tick will happen when either the bid or quote price changes by a pip of the currency pair.</p>\n<h2 id=\"the-data\"></h2>\n<p>The dataset used in this blog post was downloaded from <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"http://www.histdata.com/\">www.histdata.com</a>. The dataset consists of around 11.5 billion rows/ticks and is almost 600GB decompressed, with a total of 66 currency pairs. It covers the period May 2000 to August 2022 but is not equally distributed over this period, i.e., there are significantly more ticks in later years than in 2000, as we’ll later illustrate. This is likely more a factor of data availability than market activity, and the<a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://www.histdata.com/\"> fact collection was only “best effort.”</a> As a result, the volume of rows for any currency is likely not a good indicator of trading activity.</p>\n<p>We provide a cleaned version of the tick data for ClickHouse testing. Users looking to obtain a more recent version of the data, or continuous updates, should consult <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"http://www.histdata.com/\">www.histdata.com</a>, which provides paid updates for a nominal fee in various formats.</p>\n<p>The original data comes in a few variants, documented <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://www.histdata.com/f-a-q/data-files-detailed-specification/\">here</a>. We specifically focus on the Ascii tick data that provides the highest level of granularity. In this post, we’ll focus on using a clean version of the data and skip the download and preparation steps. For those interested, we have documented the steps to download and cleanse this dataset <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://gist.github.com/gingerwizard/d8d94edede460289b591d30f67589439\">here</a>.</p>\n<p>Our processed data consists of 5 columns, with a row per tick, containing the time to ms granularity, an indication of base and quote currency and the ask and bid quotes.</p>\n<div><pre><code><span>\"datetime\",\"bid\",\"ask\",\"base\",\"quote\"</span>\n<span>\"2022-01-02 17:03:54.650\",1.1369,1.13776,\"EUR\",\"USD\"</span>\n<span>\"2022-01-02 17:04:28.267\",1.13689,1.13776,\"EUR\",\"USD\"</span>\n<span>\"2022-01-02 17:05:07.458\",1.13692,1.13776,\"EUR\",\"USD\"</span>\n<span>\"2022-01-02 17:06:51.613\",1.13668,1.1369,\"EUR\",\"USD\"</span></code></pre></div>\n<p>It is important to emphasize that the tick data does not represent an actual trade/exchange. The number of trades/exchanges per second is significantly higher than a few per second! Nor does it capture the price that was agreed or the volume of the currency which was exchanged (logically <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://www.histdata.com/f-a-q/\">0 in the source data</a> and hence ignored). Rather it simply marks when the bid or quote price is changed by a pip for the currency pair.</p>\n<p>The data itself is distributed in 2 formats: <code>zst</code> compressed <code>csv</code> and parquet. Each set provides a file per month/year, resulting in 269 files for each. For S3, this means parallelizing both the downloading and <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/docs/en/operations/settings/settings/#input-format-parallel-parsing\">parsing of files</a> before the query is evaluated (again in parallel where possible) - interesting code comment here for more <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://github.com/ClickHouse/ClickHouse/blob/22.8/src/Processors/Formats/Impl/ParallelParsingInputFormat.h#L27\">technical readers</a>. By default, most steps will utilize the number of cores available.</p>\n<p>Each of our <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.cloud/signUp\">ClickHouse Cloud</a> nodes has eight cores. Via the setting <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/docs/en/integrations/s3/s3-optimizing-performance/#using-threads\"><code>max_download_threads</code></a>, we increase the parallelization of the first stage and download more files in parallel - at the cost of greater memory consumption.</p>\n<pre><p>\nSELECT\n    base,\n    quote,\n    avg(ask - bid) AS spread\nFROM s3('https://datasets-documentation.s3.eu-west-3.amazonaws.com/forex/csv/year_month/*.csv.zst', 'CSVWithNames')\nGROUP BY\n    base,\n    quote\nORDER BY spread ASC\nLIMIT 10\nSETTINGS max_download_threads = 12\n// result omitted for brevity\n10 rows in set. Processed 11.58 billion rows, 509.59 GB (26.59 million rows/s., 1.17 GB/s.)\n</p>\n</pre>\n<p>Any further increases in threads are unlikely to equate to performance gains as we suffer from increased switching and poorer data access patterns. Below, we look for the biggest day changes in the spread for the EUR/USD pair using a <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/docs/en/sql-reference/window-functions/#clickhouse-specific-window-functions\">window function</a>. Oil and currencies are <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://www.investopedia.com/articles/forex/092415/oil-currencies-understanding-their-correlation.asp\">inherently connected where a change in one can impact the other considerably</a>. This correlation is more noticeable for USD currency pairs, where the pair currency is tied to an economy that depends heavily on crude exports. Currencies, such as Japanese Yen, which are associated with more diverse economies, typically have a looser correlation. We can confirm this hypothesis by using the <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/docs/en/sql-reference/aggregate-functions/reference/corr/\">correlation coefficient</a> function in ClickHouse.
