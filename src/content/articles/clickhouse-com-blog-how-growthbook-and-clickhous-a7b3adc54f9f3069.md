They've identified a user need, designed something they believe is intuitive, and debated the rollout strategy. You release a new feature, usage ticks up, and the charts in your dashboard trend in the right direction. But those aggregate numbers don't tell the full story, and they often lead teams astray.</p>\n<p>\"The impact of the products we ship is surprisingly non-obvious,\" Graham says. \"We're all trying to build great products, but even with our best efforts, we actually fail a lot.\"</p>\n<p>In his presentation, he showed an A/B test from Airbnb that may have seemed like a clear win but ended up underperforming, and another from Netflix that appeared risky but turned out to be a success. Once you've optimized your core flows and eliminated low-hanging fruit, it's harder to find meaningful wins. \"The more optimized your product is, the lower your success rate becomes over time,\" Graham says.</p>\n<p>In the face of this uncertainty, A/B testing tells you, with confidence, whether what you built is working. What begins as a handful of experiments becomes dozens, then hundreds. Teams move from \"walk\" to \"run,\" as Graham puts it, testing across product areas with help from dedicated data and growth leads.</p>\n<p>At the highest level—what Graham calls ubiquitous experimentation—A/B testing becomes the default for how you launch anything. \"Every change, no matter how small, is an A/B test,\" he says. At product powerhouses like Microsoft and LinkedIn, there are hundreds of thousands of experiments running each year, often in parallel, \"down to the pixel.\"</p>\n<p>But operating at that scale requires a fundamentally different kind of system. \"When you're running five tests, the product that you build and how you scale it is entirely different from the product you build for 100,000 tests,\" Graham says.</p>\n<p>To make that level of experimentation work, you need a system that's fast, flexible, and self-serve—where any team can run tests without one team or one person being the bottleneck.
