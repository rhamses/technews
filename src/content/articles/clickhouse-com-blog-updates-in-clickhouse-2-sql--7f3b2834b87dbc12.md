updates visible immediately, without waiting for the mutation to run.</p>\n<h2 id=\"stage-2-on-the-fly-updates-for-instant-visibility\"></h2>\n<p>Classic mutations are heavyweight: they rewrite full data columns and take time to finish, especially on large datasets. To reduce the latency between issuing an UPDATE and seeing the result, ClickHouse introduced <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/docs/concepts/features/operations/update/on-fly-mutations\">on-the-fly mutations</a>, an optimization that makes updates visible immediately, even before any part is rewritten.</p>\n<blockquote>\n<p>This was a natural first step on the path to patch parts. It didn’t avoid rewrites, but it made updates feel instant.</p>\n</blockquote>\n<p>The diagram below illustrates the mechanism: the UPDATE is stored in memory and applied on read, while the actual mutation runs asynchronously in the background:</p>\n<p><span><img alt=\"Blog-updates Part 2.004.png\" src=\"https://clickhouse.com/_next/image?url=%2Fuploads%2FBlog_updates_Part_2_004_e4bdb4f086.png&w=2048&q=75\"></span></p>\n<ol>\n<li>\n<p><strong>Insert</strong>: As before, two rows are ① inserted into the orders table, ② creating the initial part <code>all_1_1_0</code>.</p>\n</li>\n<li>\n<p><strong>UPDATE issued</strong>: We ③ update the mouse row. ClickHouse stores the update expression in memory.</p>\n</li>\n<li>\n<p><strong>SELECT issued</strong>: The ④ query reads the original data part, but ClickHouse ⑤ applies the update on the fly in memory.</p>\n</li>\n<li>\n<p><strong>Result</strong>: The updated row is visible immediately in the ⑥ query result.</p>\n</li>\n<li>\n<p><strong>Mutation runs</strong>: In the background, ClickHouse ⑦ rewrites the part as <code>all_1_1_0_2</code> and drops the old one.</p>\n</li>\n</ol>\n<h3 id=\"summary-and-tradeoffs\"></h3>\n<p>On-the-fly mutations improve responsiveness, but they don’t eliminate background rewrites. They can also slow down SELECTs if many updates accumulate, and <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/docs/concepts/features/operations/update/on-fly-mutations#support-for-subqueries-and-non-deterministic-functions\">support for subqueries and non-determinism is limited</a>.</p>\n<p>This was a practical stopgap, an easier, faster optimization that gave us breathing room to build something better. The real breakthrough was still ahead: <strong>patch parts</strong>, a fundamentally different mechanism designed to handle frequent updates efficiently at scale.</p>\n<h2 id=\"stage-3-patch-parts-updates-the-clickhouse-way\"></h2>\n<p>Earlier approaches weren’t good enough, so we built something better.</p>\n<h3 id=\"why-classic-mutations-weren-t-enough\"></h3>\n<p>Even with optimizations like on-the-fly updates, the core model had limits:</p>\n<ul>\n<li>\n<p>Rewrites of entire columns, even if just a few rows have changed, which is wasteful at scale.</p>\n</li>\n<li>\n<p>Blocking behind previous merges and mutations, adding latency and unpredictability.</p>\n</li>\n</ul>\n<p>That’s why we built a new mechanism from scratch — <strong>patch parts</strong> — named for what they do:\n<strong>they patch source parts during merges, applying only the changed data.</strong></p>\n<h3 id=\"a-model-built-on-what-works\"></h3>\n<p>Patch parts borrow two proven ideas from our <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/blog/updates-in-clickhouse-1-purpose-built-engines\">specialized engines</a>, <strong>fast inserts</strong> and <strong>background merges</strong>, and generalize them, fully encapsulated for flexible, SQL-style updates:</p>\n<ol>\n<li>\n<p><strong>Fast inserts</strong>: ClickHouse handles inserts at <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/blog/updates-in-clickhouse-1-purpose-built-engines#inserts-are-so-fast-we-turned-updates-into-inserts\">high throughput</a> (e.g., in one production setup, over <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/blog/how-tesla-built-quadrillion-scale-observability-platform-on-clickhouse#proving-the-system-at-scale\">ClickHouse ingests over 1 billion rows per second</a>). We use this to model updates and deletes as lightweight inserts.</p>\n</li>\n<li>\n<p><strong>Background merges</strong>: MergeTree is already scanning and rewriting data. Applying updates or deleting rows during this process adds <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/blog/updates-in-clickhouse-1-purpose-built-engines#merges-are-fast-thanks-to-sorted-parts\">near-zero overhead</a>.</p>\n</li>\n</ol>\n<p>Let’s walk through a simple example to understand how this works. (<em>We’ll start small to build intuition, then go through the internals in the next section.)</em></p>\n<h2 id=\"how-patch-parts-work\"></h2>\n<p>Let’s understand how patch parts behave and why they’re so efficient.</p>\n<p>We’ll reuse the small orders table from earlier to walk through a simple update:</p>\n<div><pre><code><span><span>CREATE TABLE</span> orders (</span>\n<span>    order_id   Int32,</span>\n<span>    item_id    String,</span>\n<span>    quantity   UInt32,</span>\n<span>    price      <span>Decimal</span>(<span>10</span>,<span>2</span>),</span>\n<span>    discount   <span>Decimal</span>(<span>5</span>,<span>2</span>)</span>\n<span>)</span>\n<span>ENGINE <span>=</span> MergeTree</span>\n<span><span>ORDER</span> <span>BY</span> (order_id, item_id);</span></code></pre></div>\n<!-- -->\n<blockquote>\n<p><strong>UPDATEs powered by patch parts are still experimental in 25.7</strong><br>To try this out in ClickHouse 25.7, you’ll need to enable the feature manually:<br><br><code>SET allow_experimental_lightweight_update = 1;</code><br><code> ALTER TABLE orders MODIFY SETTING enable_block_number_column = 1, enable_block_offset_column = 1;</code><br><br><em>(It is expected to enter beta in 25.8)</em></p>\n</blockquote>\n<p>We’ll also reuse the same initial order from earlier: two items (kbd, mouse). We’ll explain how that works <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/blog/updates-in-clickhouse-2-sql-style-updates#how-patch-on-read-works\">shortly</a>.</p>\n<h3 id=\"a-patch-part-is-a-delta-not-a-replacement\"></h3>\n<p>Unlike classic mutations, ClickHouse doesn’t rewrite the entire column or part. Instead, it creates a new, compact <strong>patch part</strong> that contains only:</p>\n<ul>\n<li>\n<p>The <strong>changed column values</strong> (quantity = 60, discount = 0.20)</p>\n</li>\n<li>\n<p><strong>Metadata to locate the original row</strong> inside the source part</p>\n</li>\n</ul>\n<blockquote>\n<p>Think of a patch part as a “diff”: a small delta that says “update just this row, just these columns.” That’s why we call them <strong>lightweight updates</strong> in ClickHouse: they’re <strong>compact and efficient</strong>.</p>\n</blockquote>\n<h3 id=\"visual-walkthrough-how-patch-parts-update-rows\"></h3>\n<p>Let’s visualize what happens during the update from above:</p>\n<p><em>(We’ve simplified the real implementation slightly to focus on the core concept; we’ll walk through the full details in the next section.)</em></p>\n<p><span><img alt=\"Blog-updates Part 2.005.png\" src=\"https://clickhouse.com/_next/image?url=%2Fuploads%2FBlog_updates_Part_2_005_1842c7cbff.png&w=2048&q=75\"></span></p>\n<p><strong>① Original data part</strong>: <br>Contains both the keyboard and mouse orders, explicitly sorted by the table’s sorting key <code>(order_id, item_id)</code>. Each row also has a <em><a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/docs/engines/table-engines/mergetree-family/mergetree#virtual-columns\">virtual</a></em> <code>_part_offset</code> system column that reflects its position inside the part, so the part is implicitly sorted by <code>_part_offset</code> as well.</p>\n<p><strong>② Patch part</strong>: <br>Created by the UPDATE. It contains only the changed values (<code>quantity</code>, <code>discount</code>) for the second row of the source part <code>all_1_1_0</code>, plus metadata system columns: <code>_part</code> = <code>all_1_1_0</code>, <code>_part_offset</code> = <code>1</code>.</p>\n<p><strong>③ Merged result</strong>: <br>During a background merge, ClickHouse merges the original and patch part together, replacing matching rows with updated values.</p>\n<h3 id=\"they-re-compact\"></h3>\n<p>Patch parts minimize what’s written, by design:</p>\n<ul>\n<li>\n<p>Only the <strong>updated values</strong> are written.</p>\n</li>\n<li>\n<p><strong>Unchanged columns</strong> (e.g. <code>order_id</code>, <code>item_id</code>, <code>price</code>) are skipped entirely; they don’t appear in the patch part at all.\n<em>(With a specialized engine like ReplacingMergeTree, updates require re-inserting the full row, including unchanged values.)</em></p>\n</li>\n</ul>\n<h3 id=\"they-re-efficient\"></h3>\n<p>Patch parts <strong>piggyback on <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/blog/updates-in-clickhouse-1-purpose-built-engines#merges-happen-in-the-background\">merges already happening in the background</a></strong>, they hook into the process ClickHouse already runs continuously, with almost zero overhead:</p>\n<ul>\n<li>\n<p><strong>All parts are naturally aligned</strong>: the original part is sorted by the table’s sorting key (and implicitly by <code>_part_offset</code>), and the patch is sorted by <code>(_part, _part_offset)</code>.</p>\n</li>\n<li>\n<p><strong>That means merges can align rows seamlessly</strong> using <code>_part_offset</code>, without extra indexing, resorting, or rewrites.</p>\n</li>\n<li>\n<p><strong>Updates just fall into place</strong>, applied in a single, efficient merge pass: the engine simply <em>interleaves the part’s data with a single linear scan of the parts</em>, with no temp buffers or random access</p>\n</li>\n</ul>\n<p><em>(This mirrors the specialized engines, but patch parts rely on <code>_part_offset</code> instead of the sorting key.)</em></p>\n<p>We’ve intentionally simplified things slightly to focus on how patch parts work in principle, on a single update.</p>\n<p>Now let’s unfold the full picture and see how they scale.</p>\n<h2 id=\"patch-parts-at-scale-tracking-targeting-merging\"></h2>\n<p>To understand how ClickHouse supports <strong>scalable, production-grade updates</strong>, we’ll go through the internal system columns and metadata structures that power patch parts.</p>\n<blockquote>\n<p>Real-world updates rely on these internals to efficiently target rows across parts and support <strong>non-blocking</strong> updates at scale.</p>\n</blockquote>\n<p>Let’s zoom in.</p>\n<p>Suppose we want to apply a 20% discount to all items in all orders where quantity is 40 or more:</p>\n<div><pre><code><span><span>UPDATE</span> orders</span>\n<span><span>SET</span> discount <span>=</span> <span>0.2</span></span>\n<span><span>WHERE</span> quantity <span>&gt;=</span> <span>40</span>;</span></code></pre></div>\n<!-- -->\n<p>This kind of SQL statement can affect <strong>many rows across many parts</strong>. But you don’t need to worry about how. It just works.</p>\n<blockquote>\n<p><strong>Declarative updates matter</strong><br>Unlike <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/blog/updates-in-clickhouse-1-purpose-built-engines\">specialized engines</a> like ReplacingMergeTree, where you’d need to insert a new row per updated row, declarative SQL updates handle the mechanics for you. Just express your intent.</p>\n</blockquote>\n<p>To show how patch parts efficiently target rows across <strong>multiple parts</strong>, the diagram below uses an extended example with more inserts and the update above.</p>\n<blockquote>\n<p><strong>Note:</strong> To keep things focused, we <strong>dim</strong> all system columns and structures <em>not used in this example</em>.<br><br>•<strong>Orange</strong> highlights system columns that are used.<br>•<strong>Blue</strong> highlights indexes that are used.<br>•<strong>Light gray</strong> indicates unused structures.<br><br>This lets us highlight the essentials while still giving you a full view of what exists under the hood.</p>\n</blockquote>\n<p><span><img alt=\"Blog-updates Part 2.006.png\" src=\"https://clickhouse.com/_next/image?url=%2Fuploads%2FBlog_updates_Part_2_006_326f03e9ec.png&w=2048&q=75\"></span></p>\n<p><strong>① Initial order</strong>: Our base example with two items.</p>\n<p><strong>② Another order</strong>: A new <code>order_id</code>, inserted separately.</p>\n<p><strong>③ Extended order</strong>: Adds two more items to the initial order.</p>\n<p>These are just regular inserts, resulting in <strong>three data parts</strong> created over time.</p>\n<p><strong>④ Patch part</strong>: Created by the UPDATE. Contains just the new column values (discount = 0.2 for two rows) and enough metadata to target the exact rows being changed</p>\n<p><strong>⑤ Merged data part</strong>: Combines the old rows and patch values into one output part</p>\n<p><strong>Cleanup:</strong> After the merge, the original parts ① to ③ (and eventually also ④) are dropped. Only ⑤, the merged result, remains.</p>\n<p>Now let’s walk through the system columns and internal data structures sketched on the diagram above.</p>\n<h3 id=\"system-columns-in-original-rows\"></h3>\n<p>To enable patch part mechanics, every row in an original data part carries <strong>three system columns</strong>:</p>\n<div><table><thead><tr><th>System column(s)</th><th>Description and purpose</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td><code>_part_offset</code></td><td>The row’s ordinal position within its part; used to align rows during merges and apply patch updates efficiently.</td></tr><tr><td><code>_block_number</code>, <code>_block_offset</code></td><td>The insert-time <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/blog/updates-in-clickhouse-1-purpose-built-engines#how-blocks-make-up-a-part\">block number</a> and row’s offset within that block; used to locate the row after merges when <code>_part_offset</code> is no longer valid. (<em>not used in this example</em>)</td></tr></tbody></table></div>\n<p><em>(These system columns are <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/docs/engines/table-engines/mergetree-family/mergetree#virtual-columns\">virtual</a> by default. Like in the earlier simplified example, we show them materialized here for clarity; in reality, they’re only stored physically after a merge.)</em></p>\n<h3 id=\"precise-targeting-metadata-in-patch-parts\"></h3>\n<p>Each patch part row includes <strong>just enough metadata</strong> to find the row it updates:</p>\n<div><table><thead><tr><th>System column(s)</th><th>Purpose</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td><code>_part</code>, <code>_part_offset</code></td><td>Identify the original row</td></tr><tr><td><code>_block_number</code>, <code>_block_offset</code></td><td>Support tracking through merges (<em>not used here</em>)</td></tr><tr><td><code>_data_version</code></td><td>Track the update version: used to skip already-applied patches and merge patch parts with other patch parts (<em>explained later</em>)</td></tr></tbody></table></div>\n<p>The rest is just:</p>\n<div><table><thead><tr><th><code>&lt;updated columns&gt;</code></th><th>New values that replace old ones (e.g. <code>discount = 0.2</code>)</th></tr></thead></table></div>\n<h3 id=\"patch-part-indexes\"></h3>\n<p>Once the patch part exists, ClickHouse needs to know when and where to apply it. For that, it builds two lightweight indexes (per patch part), one forward, one reverse:</p>\n<div><table><thead><tr><th><code>Index name</code></th><th>Purpose</th></tr></thead><tbody><tr><td><code>Source part index</code></td><td>Maps each affected source part to the min/max data version. Used to ask: <em>Should I apply this patch to this part?</em></td></tr><tr><td><code>Reverse index</code></td><td>Maps the patch's data version to all parts and blocks that need it. Used to find patch targets even after merges.</td></tr></tbody></table></div>\n<p>These indexes enable <strong>efficient targeting</strong> even when data parts are merged in the background.</p>\n<h3 id=\"fast-source-part-matching-via-index\"></h3>\n<p>ClickHouse uses the patch part’s <code>source part index</code> to quickly determine which source parts the update applies to. In our example, only <code>all_1_1_0</code> and <code>all_3_3_0</code> match, the update doesn’t touch <code>all_2_2_0</code>.</p>\n<h3 id=\"fast-patch-merge-via-system-column-driven-sorting\"></h3>\n<p>As explained earlier, <strong><a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/blog/updates-in-clickhouse-2-sql-style-updates#theyre-efficient\">patch parts piggyback on merges already running in the background</a></strong>. Since all parts are sorted by _part_offset (either implicitly or explicitly), ClickHouse can apply the patch in a <strong>single, efficient merge pass</strong>.</p>\n<h3 id=\"preserving-row-identity-during-merges\"></h3>\n<p>In the merged part (⑤), <code>_part_offset</code> values are <strong>recreated</strong> to reflect each row’s new position. But <code>_block_number</code> and <code>_block_offset</code> are <strong>copied over unchanged</strong> from the original parts.</p>\n<blockquote>\n<p>This detail is <strong>crucial</strong> for supporting updates that run <strong>concurrently with merges</strong> of the affected parts.</p>\n</blockquote>\n<p>Let’s explore how that works in the next section.</p>\n<h2 id=\"updates-don-t-wait-for-merges\"></h2>\n<p>ClickHouse updates are <strong>non-blocking</strong>: they don’t wait for merges to finish. Instead, each update runs against a <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/blog/clickhouse-release-25-06#single-snapshot-for-select\">snapshot</a> of the parts that exist when the UPDATE begins.</p>\n<blockquote>\n<p>Classic mutations must wait for prior merges and mutations to finish before they run.</p>\n</blockquote>\n<p>In most cases, that snapshot is still valid when the patch part is later applied. But if those parts are <strong>merged away</strong> before that happens, ClickHouse automatically falls back to a different matching strategy.</p>\n<p>Let’s look at how that fallback works.</p>\n<h3 id=\"what-happens-if-the-parts-are-merged-before-a-patch-is-applied\"></h3>\n<p>The next diagram picks up the same extended example from the previous section, but shows what happens when the parts are merged <strong>before</strong> the patch is applied.</p>\n<blockquote>\n<p>In the previous diagram, we dimmed <code>_block_number</code> and <code>_block_offset</code>. This time, we dim (show in light gray) <code>_part</code>, <code>_part_offset</code>, and the patch’s <code>source part index</code>; they’re not used here.</p>\n</blockquote>\n<p><span><img alt=\"Blog-updates Part 2.007.png\" src=\"https://clickhouse.com/_next/image?url=%2Fuploads%2FBlog_updates_Part_2_007_4ed2800064.png&w=2048&q=75\"></span></p>\n<p><strong>What changes in this scenario?</strong></p>\n<p><strong>①–③</strong>: <strong>The same inserts</strong> and ⑤ UPDATE run as before, those steps are unchanged.</p>\n<p><strong>④</strong>: But this time, <strong>a background merge</strong> combines the original parts into a single part before the patch can be applied. The source parts (①–③) are then removed, as usual for merges.</p>\n<p><strong>⑤</strong>: The original <code>_part</code> and <code>_part_offset</code> values referenced by the patch part are no longer valid. So ClickHouse falls back to <code>_block_number</code> and <code>_block_offset</code>, which are preserved during merges.</p>\n<p><strong>⑥–⑧</strong>: <strong>ClickHouse applies the patch using a hash join</strong> based on those preserved values, producing a merged data part with the patch applied.</p>\n<p>Let’s take a closer look at how that fallback patching path works.</p>\n<h3 id=\"source-part-matching-via-reverse-index\"></h3>\n<p>The reverse index shows that this patch applies to <strong>data version 4</strong>, and targets parts <code>all_1_1_0</code> and <code>all_3_3_0</code>, which (based on their <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/blog/updates-in-clickhouse-1-purpose-built-engines#how-to-read-a-part-name\">names</a>) contained <strong>block numbers 1 and 3</strong>.</p>\n<p>The merged part <code>all_1_3_1</code> (based on its name) spans <strong>block numbers 1 to 3</strong>, so it’s a valid match. Also, its data version is <code>1</code> (inferred from its lowest block number), which is less than the patch’s version <code>4</code>, so the patch can be applied.</p>\n<p>This reverse mapping lets ClickHouse match and patch data even after merges invalidate the original part names.</p>\n<h3 id=\"patch-application-via-join-on-block-based-system-columns\"></h3>\n<p>We can’t use <code>_part</code> and <code>_part_offset</code> anymore to efficiently apply the patch in a merge, since the source parts are gone and their original values no longer match the corresponding rows in the merged output.</p>\n<p>Instead, ClickHouse applies the patch using a <strong>hash join-based algorithm</strong>, loading the patch part into memory as a hash table keyed by <code>(_block_number, _block_offset)</code>, and joins it with the merged part using the same key to update the rows.</p>\n<p>This fallback is slower and more memory-hungry than the fast path in the previous section. The patch must fit entirely in memory, but in the future, ClickHouse may support a <code>full merge join</code> that isn’t memory-bound, though that’s not yet implemented.</p>\n<p>Thankfully, this rarely happens in practice, since the source data parts usually live long enough for fast patching.</p>\n<p>Now that we’ve seen how ClickHouse handles UPDATEs, even during merges, let’s talk about how DELETEs have gotten just as efficient.</p>\n<h2 id=\"lightweight-deletes-are-now-even-lighter\"></h2>\n<p>In <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/blog/updates-in-clickhouse-2-sql-style-updates#stage-15-lightweight-deletes-still-mutations-but-faster\">Stage 1.5</a>, <strong>lightweight DELETEs</strong> already gave us a win: they rewrote only the <code>_row_exists</code> deletion mask via an ALTER UPDATE, avoiding full-row rewrites.</p>\n<p>But we can go even lighter.</p>\n<p>When <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/docs/operations/settings/settings#lightweight_delete_mode\"><code>lightweight_delete_mode</code></a> = <code>lightweight_update</code>, DELETEs aren’t ALTERs at all. ClickHouse simply creates a patch part that sets <code>_row_exists = 0</code> for the deleted rows. The rows are then dropped during the next background merge.</p>\n<p>The diagram below reuses the same example from Stage 1.5: we delete the mouse item from the original order. (<em>Unused system columns like _block_number, _block_offset, etc. are omitted for clarity.</em>)</p>\n<p><span><img alt=\"Blog-updates Part 2.008.png\" src=\"https://clickhouse.com/_next/image?url=%2Fuploads%2FBlog_updates_Part_2_008_1825514794.png&w=2048&q=75\"></span></p>\n<p>① The initial part contains two rows.</p>\n<p>② A patch part sets <code>_row_exists = 0</code> for the deleted row (the mouse).</p>\n<p>③ During the next merge, that row disappears.</p>\n<p>ClickHouse applies the patch instantly. Until the merge happens, queries simply ignore rows where <code>_row_exists = 0</code>. This is the heart of <strong>patch-on-read query execution</strong>.</p>\n<h2 id=\"how-patch-on-read-works\"></h2>\n<p>ClickHouse doesn’t wait to materialize patch parts before returning updated results. Instead:</p>\n<blockquote>\n<p>Patch parts are applied automatically and on the fly in memory during query execution, <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/blog/updates-in-clickhouse-1-purpose-built-engines#getting-up-to-date-results-with-final\">like an implicit FINAL</a>.</p>\n</blockquote>\n<p><strong>This patch-on-read mechanism is designed to minimize performance impact.</strong></p>\n<p>Usually, the data selected for a query (after <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/docs/primary-indexes\">index analysis</a>) is located in several <strong>data ranges</strong> (consecutive blocks of rows) in several data parts. These ranges are dynamically spread by the <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/docs/academic_overview#4-query-processing-layer\">query engine</a> across ① separate and <strong>parallel stream stages</strong> (data streams) and then processed by ② <strong><a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/docs/optimize/query-parallelism#distributing-work-across-processing-lanes\">parallel processing lanes</a></strong> that filter, aggregate, sort, and limit the data into its final result:</p>\n<p><span><img alt=\"Blog-updates Part 2.009.png\" src=\"https://clickhouse.com/_next/image?url=%2Fuploads%2FBlog_updates_Part_2_009_bfefb2e8c3.png&w=2048&q=75\"></span></p>\n<p>Not-yet-merged patch parts are ③ matched and <strong>applied independently for each data range</strong> in each data stream, ensuring that updates are applied correctly without disrupting parallelism.</p>\n<p><em>(If needed, you can fully materialize all accumulated not-yet-merged changes with <code>ALTER TABLE … APPLY PATCHES</code>, but that’s optional.)</em></p>\n<p>To fully understand patch parts, we also need to look at their lifecycle: how ClickHouse merges, deduplicates, and cleans them up in the background.</p>\n<h2 id=\"what-happens-to-patch-parts-over-time\"></h2>\n<p>Patch parts may seem special, but under the hood, they’re just regular parts in ClickHouse. That means:</p>\n<ul>\n<li>\n<p>They are <strong>merged with other patch parts</strong> using the <em><a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/blog/updates-in-clickhouse-1-purpose-built-engines#replacingmergetree-replace-rows-by-inserting-new-ones\">ReplacingMergeTree</a></em> algorithm, with <code>_data_version</code> as a <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/docs/engines/table-engines/mergetree-family/replacingmergetree#replacingmergetree-parameters\">version column</a>. This ensures each patch part stores only the latest version of each updated row.</p>\n</li>\n<li>\n<p>They’re <strong>automatically cleaned up</strong> once their changes are fully materialized into all affected data parts, or when merged with another patch part. Background cleanup threads handle this safely.</p>\n</li>\n<li>\n<p><strong><a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/docs/operations/settings/merge-tree-settings#parts_to_throw_insert\">They count toward the TOO_MANY_PARTS threshold</a></strong>, which applies <strong>per partition</strong>. To mitigate this, patch parts are stored in <strong>separate partitions based on the set of updated columns</strong>. So if you run multiple UPDATE statements that affect different columns, like <code>SET x = …</code>, <code>SET y = …</code>, and <code>SET x = …, y = …</code>, you’ll get separate patch partitions, each with its own part count.</p>\n</li>\n</ul>\n<p>This design keeps patch parts fast, efficient, and deeply integrated with MergeTree mechanics.</p>\n<p>So far, we’ve focused on how patch parts behave in isolation. But what happens when multiple updates arrive at the same time? Let’s look at how ClickHouse coordinates concurrent updates safely.</p>\n<h2 id=\"coordinating-concurrent-updates\"></h2>\n<p><strong>ClickHouse runs updates in parallel by default.</strong> If two updates touch the same columns, it automatically runs them in the correct order. You don’t need to configure anything; it just works.</p>\n<p>You can tweak this behavior with a few settings:</p>\n<ul>\n<li>\n<p><strong><a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/docs/operations/settings/settings#update_parallel_mode\"><code>update_parallel_mode</code></a></strong>:</p>\n<ul>\n<li>\n<p><code>auto</code> (default): Serializes dependent updates (e.g. <code>UPDATE a=3 WHERE b=2</code> and <code>UPDATE b=2 WHERE a=1</code>). Runs others in parallel.</p>\n</li>\n<li>\n<p><code>sync</code>: Runs all updates one at a time.</p>\n</li>\n<li>\n<p><code>async</code>: Runs all updates with no coordination.</p>\n</li>\n</ul>\n</li>\n<li>\n<p><strong><a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/docs/operations/settings/settings#update_sequential_consistency\"><code>update_sequential_consistency</code></a></strong> (off by default): Ensures each update sees the latest visible state, at some performance cost.</p>\n</li>\n</ul>\n<p>But for most workloads, the default is fast, safe, and does the right thing.</p>\n<p>All of this coordination happens behind the scenes, so that from the outside, updates just feel like SQL. It just works.</p>\n<p>As we reach the end of this post, let’s step back and look at the bigger picture.</p>\n<h2 id=\"from-engine-specific-tricks-to-familiar-sql\"></h2>\n<p>Patch parts bring efficient SQL-style UPDATEs to ClickHouse, not by breaking the rules of columnar storage, but by embracing them.</p>\n<p>We leaned into what makes ClickHouse fast:<br>\n<strong>Inserts are fast. Merges are continuous. Parts are immutable and sorted.</strong></p>\n<p>And because <strong>inserts are so fast</strong>, we turned updates into inserts.<br>\nClickHouse inserts compact <strong>patch parts</strong> behind the scenes and applies them efficiently during merges.</p>\n<p><strong>Merges are already happening, so we made them do more. Without really doing more.</strong><br>\nSince the engine is already merging data parts in the background, it now applies updates with minimal overhead.</p>\n<p><strong>Patch parts slide in instantly with minimal impact on query performance.</strong><br>\nUpdates show up right away, with not-yet-merged patches applied in a way that preserves parallelism.</p>\n<p>This is the core mechanism that now powers <em>lightweight updates</em> in ClickHouse.</p>\n<p>They build on the same principles as engines like ReplacingMergeTree, but in a fully general way, encapsulated behind flexible, standard SQL syntax:</p>\n<div><pre><code><span><span>UPDATE</span> orders</span>\n<span><span>SET</span> discount <span>=</span> <span>0.2</span></span>\n<span><span>WHERE</span> quantity <span>&gt;=</span> <span>40</span>;</span></code></pre></div>\n<!-- -->\n<p>The result: Updates show up instantly. Queries stay fast. Nothing blocks. It’s UPDATE, the ClickHouse way. All in familiar SQL.</p>\n<p><strong>In <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/blog/updates-in-clickhouse-3-benchmarks\">Part 3</a>, we’ll put it to the test.</strong><br>\nWe benchmark every update method, mutations, on-the-fly mutations, and lightweight patch parts updates, and show just how fast standard SQL UPDATEs in ClickHouse can get.<br>\n<strong>Spoiler: it’s not just faster. It’s up to 1,000× faster. Sometimes even more.</strong></p></div></article>"
