Given a heatmap of slow or erroring spans, it surfaces which attribute values appear disproportionately in a selected region compared to the background. This is critical for SREs and developers investigating production issues, allowing them to quickly identify the attributes and correlated span characteristics driving performance degradation or errors. Instead of manually slicing data or forming complex queries, Event Deltas highlights the signals that matter, accelerating the path from symptom to root cause. For a deeper dive into how the feature works, see our <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/blog/faster-root-cause-for-slow-traces-with-clickstack-event-deltas\">dedicated blog post</a>.</p>\n<p>This month, we made four significant improvements across how the feature behaves.</p>\n<blockquote>\n<p>To play with these changes, we recommend heading over to our <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://play-clickstack.clickhouse.com/search?source=3b743139&where=&select=&whereLanguage=lucene&filters=%255B%255D&orderBy=&mode=delta&isLive=false&from=1775654690000&to=1775741090000&xMin=1775666834.0190363&xMax=1775719838.3538175&yMin=1.7378845061203285&yMax=913.6388954631163\">demo environment </a>where you can experiment with Event Deltas on the <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/docs/use-cases/observability/clickstack/getting-started/remote-demo-data\">OpenTelemetry demo dataset</a> yourself.</p>\n</blockquote>\n<h3 id=\"always-on-attribute-distribution\"></h3>\n<p>Previously, the attribute distribution charts were only populated after you selected an area on the heatmap to compare that selection against the background spans. This allows you to immediately spot dominant behaviors across the dataset without needing to make a selection first - even when the heatmap is stable and shows no obvious changes.</p>\n<p>When a user selects a region, the chart switches to comparison mode, showing red and green bars contrasting the selected group against the overall distribution. We’ll follow up with a dedicated blog post exploring our vision for it and how we think can simplify investigative workflows in ClickStack.</p>\n</blockquote>\n<p><span><img alt=\"event_deltas_no_selection.png\" src=\"https://clickhouse.com/_next/image?url=%2Fuploads%2Fevent_deltas_no_selection_fb3d54050a.png&w=2048&q=75\"></span></p>\n<p>When you click and drag to select a region, the chart transitions to comparison mode and shows red and green bars contrasting the selected group against the background.</p>\n<p><span><img alt=\"event_deltas_selection.png\" src=\"https://clickhouse.com/_next/image?url=%2Fuploads%2Fevent_deltas_selection_36818f5a3f.png&w=2048&q=75\"></span></p>\n<h3 id=\"smarter-attribute-sorting\"></h3>\n<p>Ranking attributes by diagnostic usefulness is harder than it looks. The previous implementation sorted by maximum raw delta, which produced misleading rankings when the outlier and inlier groups had different sample sizes. An attribute with identical distribution in both groups could still rank highly if the raw counts differed.</p>\n<p>For example, imagine <code>service.name = checkout</code> appears 1,000 times in the background and 100 times in the selected region, while <code>service.name = payments</code> appears 10 times in the background and 8 times in the selected region. Despite both having very similar proportional distributions, the raw delta for checkout is much larger, causing it to rank higher even though it is not more indicative of the issue. In practice, this meant attributes with high absolute counts were often prioritised over those that were actually more strongly correlated with the anomaly.</p>\n<p>Instead, we now use proportional comparison scoring, which normalises each group's percentages to sum to 100% before computing the delta. Attributes with identical proportional distributions score zero regardless of how the raw counts differ, making the ranking far more reliable when groups are unequal in size.</p>\n<h3 id=\"filter-exclude-and-copy-from-attribute-bars\"></h3>\n<p>The real value of Event Deltas is not just identifying outliers, but explaining why they differ from normal behavior. By comparing a selected subset of spans, the foreground or outliers, against the baseline distribution, the background or inliers, it surfaces which attribute values are disproportionately associated with degraded performance. This removes the need for the manual, iterative workflow of filtering, grouping, and visually comparing distributions across columns.</p>\n<p>However, historically, once users identified a suspicious attribute, the next step was still a little cumbersome. Translating that insight into a concrete query, validating it, and continuing the investigation required multiple manual steps.</p>\n<p>To address this, clicking any bar in the attribute comparison chart now opens an action popover with three options: include the value, exclude it, or copy it to the clipboard.</p>\n<p><span><img alt=\"event_deltas_filtering.png\" src=\"https://clickhouse.com/_next/image?url=%2Fuploads%2Fevent_deltas_filtering_18ad04cea0.png&w=2048&q=75\"></span></p>\n<p>This allows users to immediately act on what Event Deltas reveals. For example, above a subset of slow spans shows that 93 percent of outliers involve <code>card_type = Visa</code>, while the background is dominated by other card types, this strongly suggests a Visa-specific issue. Including the value lets users isolate and inspect only those problematic spans to confirm the hypothesis. Excluding it helps verify whether performance returns to normal without that factor, or reveals secondary outliers that were previously masked.</p>\n<h3 id=\"deterministic-sampling\"></h3>\n<p>Event Deltas samples spans when building the heatmap. Previously this used <code>ORDER BY rand()</code>, which meant hovering over the same region across two renders could highlight different cells, making the interface feel unstable. Sampling now uses <code>ORDER BY cityHash64(SpanId)</code>, so the same query always selects the same subset of spans and the heatmap behaves consistently across interactions - important to ensure analysis is repeatable.</p>\n<h2 id=\"sql-charts-with-grafana-style-macros\"></h2>\n<p>Query builders are great for getting started and covering the common path, but they are inherently opinionated. As queries become more complex, joining across datasets, applying custom aggregations, or expressing nuanced logic, users often need direct access to SQL to fully exploit the underlying power of ClickHouse. Supporting this balance between simplicity and flexibility has been a key focus for ClickStack.</p>\n<p>Over the past several months, we have been steadily expanding support for raw SQL charts across all visualisation types. This month, we completed the last two chart types and significantly deepened the feature set for users writing SQL directly.</p>\n<p>Raw SQL is now available for number tiles, pie charts, line charts, table charts, and stacked bar charts - expanding on support for tables last month. Users writing their own queries can now use the full expressiveness of ClickHouse to drive any visualisation, not just the ones the query builder supports.</p>\n<p><span><img alt=\"sql-charts.png\" src=\"https://clickhouse.com/_next/image?url=%2Fuploads%2Fsql_charts_0dc33889ab.png&w=2048&q=75\"></span></p>\n<p>SQL charts bring a high degree of flexibility, allowing users to express complex queries beyond the constraints of the query builder. At the same time, they still need to integrate seamlessly with the rest of the dashboard experience, particularly respecting global filters and time ranges. Without this, SQL charts quickly become disconnected from the surrounding context.</p>\n<p>To address this, we have introduced a set of template macros that expand at query time, following conventions familiar to Grafana users. These allow users to write expressive SQL while automatically inheriting the dashboard’s current time window and active filters, ensuring consistency across all visualisations.</p>\n<p><code>$__timeFilter(column)</code> expands to a seconds-precision range predicate on the named column. <code>$__timeFilter_ms(column)</code> does the same at millisecond precision. <code>$__timeInterval(column)</code> produces a <code>toStartOfInterval</code> expression for use in a <code>GROUP BY</code>, automatically aligned to the selected time window granularity.</p>\n<p><code>$__filters</code> is replaced at query time with the active dashboard filter predicates. When no filters are active, it expands to <code>1=1</code> so the query remains valid without any conditional logic. A source must be selected on the chart for filter introspection to work.</p>\n<p>A typical raw SQL chart using these macros might look like this:</p></div><div><pre><code><span><span>SELECT</span></span>\n<span>  toStartOfInterval(<span>Timestamp</span>, <span>INTERVAL</span> {intervalSeconds:Int64} <span>second</span>) <span>AS</span> ts, <span>-- (Timestamp column)</span></span>\n<span>  ServiceName,                                                                   </span>\n<span>  <span>avg</span>(Duration)                                                                        </span>\n<span><span>FROM</span> $__sourceTable</span>\n<span><span>WHERE</span> <span>Timestamp</span> <span>&gt;=</span> fromUnixTimestamp64Milli ({startDateMilliseconds:Int64})</span>\n<span>  <span>AND</span> <span>Timestamp</span> <span>&lt;</span> fromUnixTimestamp64Milli ({endDateMilliseconds:Int64})</span>\n<span>  <span>AND</span> $__filters</span>\n<span><span>GROUP</span> <span>BY</span> ServiceName, ts</span></code></pre></div><div><p>The macro <code>$__sourceTable</code> resolves to the fully qualified table name of the selected source at query time. This is particularly useful for import and export workflows. When a dashboard containing raw SQL charts is exported and imported into a different environment, the source mapping applied during import is automatically substituted into the macro. There is no need to manually update table references after importing.</p>\n<blockquote>\n<p>The macro also accepts a metric-type argument: <code>$__sourceTable(sum)</code> resolves to the appropriate metric table for sum-type aggregations, which is necessary when querying OpenTelemetry metric tables which are partitioned by aggregation type.</p>\n</blockquote>\n<p>Raw SQL charts are also now fully supported in the external API across all five display types: line, number, pie, table, and stacked bar. Teams managing dashboards programmatically through the API can now create and update SQL-backed tiles using the same endpoints used for builder-based charts.</p>\n<h2 id=\"aggregations-for-sampled-trace-data\"></h2>\n<p>As observability datasets grow, many high-throughput systems turn to sampling to control storage and ingestion costs. Both head-based and tail-based sampling follow the same principle: retain only a subset of spans while discarding the rest. Head sampling makes decisions early, often probabilistically or via simple rules, while tail sampling evaluates full traces before deciding what to keep. Although these approaches differ in execution, they share a common outcome: the stored dataset is no longer complete.</p>\n<p>In ClickHouse, sampling is often unnecessary for many workloads due to high compression and strong query performance at scale. However, at very large volumes, some users still choose to sample. The challenge is that once data is sampled, standard aggregations become incorrect. Counts underestimate true volume, averages become biased toward retained spans, and percentiles no longer reflect the full distribution. In short, you lose statistical accuracy unless the sampling is accounted for during query execution.</p>\n<p>To address this, ClickStack introduces sample-aware aggregations at the source level. Since sampling is a property of the dataset, we allow users to define how to interpret it directly in the trace source configuration. This is done via a sampleRateExpression, which evaluates to the per-span sampling rate, typically stored alongside each span, for example <code>SpanAttributes['SampleRate']</code>. This allows sampling rates to vary across spans while still being handled consistently.</p>\n<p><span><img alt=\"sampling_config.png\" src=\"https://clickhouse.com/_next/image?url=%2Fuploads%2Fsampling_config_dd92ff26b3.png&w=2048&q=75\"></span></p>\n<p>Once configured, ClickStack automatically rewrites aggregations to account for sampling. A count() becomes <code>sum(weight)</code>, where the weight represents the inverse sampling rate. Averages are computed as weighted averages with null-safe division, sums are scaled accordingly, and percentile calculations use quantileTDigestWeighted to preserve distribution accuracy. The weight itself is computed as <code>greatest(toUInt64OrZero(toString(expr)), 1)</code>, ensuring that missing or invalid sampling rates default safely.</p>\n<p>This correction is applied transparently across dashboards, alerts, the external API, and AI-powered summarisation. By defining sampling once at the source level, users can continue to build charts and queries as usual, with ClickStack ensuring the results remain statistically correct.</p>\n<p><span><img alt=\"sampled_chart.png\" src=\"https://clickhouse.com/_next/image?url=%2Fuploads%2Fsampled_chart_b48837ebf0.png&w=2048&q=75\"></span></p>\n<p><em>A simple count on a sampled dataset maps to a sum on the sample rate - <code>sum(greatest(toUInt64OrZero(toString(SpanAttributes['SampleRate'])), 1))</code></em></p>\n<h2 id=\"dashboard-template-gallery-and-listing-pages\"></h2>\n<p>As ClickStack usage grows, so does the number of dashboards teams create. Favorites are stored per user, so each team member maintains their own independently.</p>\n<h2 id=\"dashboard-filter-improvements\"></h2>\n<p>Last year, we introduced <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://clickhouse.com/blog/whats-new-in-clickstack-october-2025#dashboard-filtering\">dashboard filters</a> to provide a simple way to apply global filtering across all charts in a dashboard. Users can now create, edit, and save dashboards and searches that persist across sessions, with no server or database required. Throughput units have also been extended to cover common operational metrics such as requests, operations, reads, writes, and IOPS, with both per-second and per-minute variants.</p>\n<p><span><img alt=\"new_metrics.png\" src=\"https://clickhouse.com/_next/image?url=%2Fuploads%2Fnew_metrics_c4e0e4f0ef.png&w=2048&q=75\"></span></p>\n<p>Existing formats like number, currency, percentage, and time remain available under a unified “Basic” group, with time continuing to support granular units down to microseconds and nanoseconds.</p></div></div></article>"
