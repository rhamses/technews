contentHtml: "<div><h2>Ingestion Woes</h2><p>First things are usually first. Often, the discourse around data engineering centers on high-performance tools and the latest AI benchmarks. However, when it comes to data engineering, getting your data to work is non-trivial and requires more engineering effort than you expect.&#160;&#160;</p><p>Most teams treat ingestion as an afterthought, building one-off solutions that don't scale. When a source changes its API, when a partner misses a delivery, when you need to backfill historical data, you're stuck debugging custom code instead of leveraging proven patterns.&#160;</p><p>Solving ingestion from source systems by hand rolling your own solution is a data engineering right of passage. You should try to do it at least once, and you will gain a greater appreciation for managed solutions like Fivetran and open-source ones like Sling and dlt.&#160;</p><h2>Why Ingestion Patterns Matter</h2><h3>The Foundation of Everything</h3><p>Data ingestion is the entry point for your entire data platform. Every downstream operation: transformation, modeling, analytics, machine learning, and AI depends on data arriving reliably, on time, and in the right format. Get ingestion wrong, and you're fighting fires downstream forever.</p><p>What makes it tricky is that you are often more beholden to systems that don't have analytics in mind. Data APIs that aren't idempotent, inconsistent authentication processes, and figuring out how deletes are handled. Because of this, you, as the data engineer, need to engineer solutions that work around these constraints to provide high-quality data to downstream processes.&#160;</p><h3>The Three Fundamental Patterns</h3><p>Most data ingestion approaches fall into three fundamental patterns: <strong>Push</strong>, <strong>Pull</strong>, and <strong>Poll</strong>. Each reflects a different approach to data flow, ownership, and operational responsibility. I created an example projec using these different methods <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://github.com/dagster-io/dagster/tree/master/examples/ingestion-patterns\">here</a>. </p><p><strong>Push-based ingestion:</strong> The source system initiates the transfer. You're the passive receiver. This approach works well when you have contractual agreements with data providers, but it relinquishes control over timing and volume. The most common pattern is for a vendor to dump data into durable object storage, such as Amazon S3.&#160;</p><p><strong>Pull-based ingestion:</strong> Your platform initiates the transfer. You control <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://docs.dagster.io/concepts/automation/schedules\">schedules</a>, data windows, and backfills. This is the default for most data engineering teams, but it requires source systems to expose APIs or support queries.</p><p><strong>Polling-based ingestion:</strong> You frequently check for new data, requesting only changes since the last check. This enables near real-time architectures but adds complexity around state management and duplicate handling.</p><p>Most of the time, the source system you are working with determines which pattern you are going to use&#160;&#160;</p><h3>Why This Matters Now</h3><p>Modern data platforms ingest from dozens of sources: SaaS tools, partner databases, internal applications, and streaming services. Without consistent patterns, you end up with:</p><p>- <strong>Inconsistent error handling:</strong> Some sources retry automatically, others fail silently</p><p>- <strong>No visibility:</strong> Different monitoring approaches for each source</p><p>- <strong>Maintenance burden:</strong> Custom code for every integration</p><p>- <strong>Data quality issues:</strong> Inconsistent validation and schema management</p><p>The right pattern, applied consistently, gives you reliability, observability, and maintainability. Having the wrong or no pattern can lead to technical debt and operational headaches.</p><h2>Understanding Push, Pull, and Poll</h2><h3>Push-Based Ingestion</h3><p>In push-based ingestion, the data producer initiates the transfer, sending data to your platform without an explicit request. The source system controls timing, volume, and format.</p><p><strong>When to use push:</strong></p><p>- You have contractual agreements with data providers</p><p>- Sources can deliver data via webhooks or direct API calls</p><p>- Real-time or near-real-time delivery is required</p><p>- You want to minimize compute costs (source pays for delivery)</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong></p><p>- Immediate delivery when data is available</p><p>- Source system controls timing and cadence</p><p>- Lower compute costs (no polling overhead)</p><p>- Works well for event-driven architectures</p><p><strong>Challenges:</strong></p><p>- Less control over timing and volume</p><p>- Must handle bursts and spikes gracefully</p><p>- Schema drift from source changes</p><p>- Requires robust error handling and idempotency</p><h3>Pull-Based Ingestion</h3><p>Pull-based ingestion is controlled by your platform. You initiate the import process, requesting data from the source at regular intervals or on demand.</p><p><strong>When to use pull:</strong></p><p>- You need control over schedules and data windows</p><p>- Source systems provide APIs or support direct queries</p><p>- Historical backfills and retroactive corrections are common</p><p>- Data freshness requirements are predictable</p><p><strong>Advantages</strong>:</p><p>- Full control over timing, volume, and scope</p><p>- Easy to implement backfills and replays</p><p>- Standardized scheduling and error handling</p><p>- Works well with batch processing architectures</p><p><strong>Challenges:</strong></p><p>- Must handle duplicate data if reprocessing</p><p>- Risk of missing records due to scheduling issues</p><p>- Requires explicit tracking of extracted data</p><p>- Source system must be queryable or expose APIs</p><h3>Polling-Based Ingestion</h3><p>Polling combines push and pull: you frequently check for new data, but request only changes or updates since the last check rather than entire datasets.</p><p><strong>When to use polling:</strong></p><p>- Near real-time or event-driven responsiveness is required</p><p>- Working with message queues (Kafka, Pulsar) or change data capture (CDC)</p><p>- Need precise control over data flow with low latency</p><p>- Source systems support incremental queries or event streams</p><p><strong>Advantages:</strong></p><p>- Responsive architectures with low end-to-end latency</p><p>- Precise control over data flow</p><p>- Efficient processing (only new data)</p><p>- Works well with streaming and event-driven architectures</p><p><strong>Challenges</strong>:</p><p>- Must reliably record state (offsets, timestamps, markers)</p><p>- Handle missed or duplicate messages</p><p>- Manage variations in poll intervals or failures</p><p>- More complex than simple pull patterns</p><h2>Practical Implementation: Building Ingestion Patterns with delivery)</p><p><strong>Trade-offs:</strong> Less control over timing, must handle bursts, requires robust error handling.</p><h3>When to Use Pull</h3><p>Choose pull when:</p><p>- You need full control over schedules and data windows</p><p>- Source systems provide APIs or support direct queries</p><p>- Historical backfills and retroactive corrections are common</p><p>- Data freshness requirements are predictable</p><p><strong>Trade-offs:</strong> Must handle duplicates, risk of missing records, requires explicit tracking.</p><h3>When to Use Poll</h3><p>Choose poll when:</p><p>- Near real-time or event-driven responsiveness is required</p><p>- Working with message queues or change data capture</p><p>- Need precise control over data flow with low latency</p><p>- Source systems support incremental queries or event streams</p><p><strong>Trade-offs:</strong> More complex state management, must handle duplicates, requires reliable checkpointing.</p><h3>Hybrid Approaches</h3><p>Most production systems use multiple patterns:</p><p>- <strong>Push for real-time events:</strong> Webhooks, streaming data</p><p>- <strong>Pull for batch processing:</strong> Scheduled extracts, API polling</p><p>- <strong>Poll for event streams:</strong> Kafka, CDC, message queues</p><p>The key is consistency: use the same error handling, observability, and idempotency patterns across all approaches.</p><h2>Best Practices</h2><h3>Idempotency: The Foundation of Reliable Ingestion</h3><p>Idempotency is one of those words you see written down but never said. Anyway, it's the guarantee that processing the same input multiple times yields identical results is essential regardless of the ingestion pattern. Without it, retries, out-of-order delivery, or operator-initiated reprocessing can corrupt downstream data.</p><p><strong>How to implement:</strong></p><p>- Track natural or synthetic keys (transaction IDs, timestamps)</p><p>- Use window boundaries for batch processing</p><p>- Implement deduplication logic that excludes already-processed records</p><p>- Test idempotency explicitly: run the same ingestion twice and verify identical results</p><p><strong>Common pitfall</strong>: Assuming your source system guarantees uniqueness. Always implement idempotency at the ingestion layer, even if the source claims to be idempotent.</p><h3>Schema Management</h3><p>Schema drift is the unexpected format changes from source systems that breaks downstream pipelines. Proactive schema management and data quality checks minimize the disruption.</p><p><strong>How to implement:</strong></p><p>- Validate schemas at ingestion time</p><p>- Use schema registries for contract enforcement</p><p>- Log schema changes and alert on unexpected modifications</p><p>- Version your schemas and handle migrations gracefully</p><p><strong>Common pitfall:</strong> Assuming schemas never change. They always do, especially with external sources.</p><h3>Observability and Monitoring</h3><p>You can't fix what you can't see. Detailed logging, metric tracking, and failure alerting should cover all ingestion stages.</p><p><strong>What to monitor:</strong></p><p>- Ingestion volume and latency</p><p>- Error rates and failure modes</p><p>- Schema validation failures</p><p>- Duplicate detection rates</p><p>- Source system availability</p><p><strong>Common pitfall:</strong> Only monitoring success/failure. Monitor data quality, latency, and volume trends to catch issues before they become problems.</p><h3>Error Handling and Recovery</h3><p>Ingestion failures are inevitable. Design for graceful degradation and easy recovery.</p><p><strong>How to implement:</strong></p><p>- Implement exponential backoff for retries</p><p>- Use dead-letter queues for unprocessable messages</p><p>- Support manual backfills and replays</p><p>- Log all failures with sufficient context for debugging</p><p><strong>Common pitfall:</strong> Failing silently or losing data on errors. Always log failures and provide recovery mechanisms to address them.</p><h2>Building Reliable Ingestion</h2><p>Data ingestion patterns ultimately shape your operational model, your ability to scale, and your team's quality of life. Push, pull, and poll each have their place, and the best platforms use all three strategically.</p><p>The pattern you choose determines how much complexity you own versus how much you push to source systems.
