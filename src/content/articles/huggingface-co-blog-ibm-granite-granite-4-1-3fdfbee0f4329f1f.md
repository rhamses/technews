To ensure the model natively handles long sequences without degrading short-context performance, we do a model merge after each LCE stage. To address this, we apply a rigorous LLM‑as‑Judge framework alongside rule‑based filtering to curate high-quality samples. Together, the pipeline automatically assess each sample against structural, semantic, and behavioral criteria, fixing issues when possible and filtering out samples that fail to meet our quality standards.</p>\n<p><a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://cdn-uploads.huggingface.co/production/uploads/6658c911e238275ea9efc339/_-jA1WScOVuIFl20cLEPU.png\"><img src=\"https://cdn-uploads.huggingface.co/production/uploads/6658c911e238275ea9efc339/_-jA1WScOVuIFl20cLEPU.png\" alt=\"SFT Data Quality Pipeline\"></a></p>\n<p><em><strong>Figure 4:</strong> The SFT data quality pipeline. Raw conversation data passes through an LLM-as-Judge with a multi-dimensional rubric, producing accept/borderline/reject verdicts. Hard-reject defects (hallucination, false premise, incorrect computation) trigger automatic rejection regardless of score.</em></p>\n<p>Our rigorous LLM‑as‑Judge framework evaluates only assistant responses, treating system prompts, user inputs, retrieved documents, and tool outputs strictly as contextual information. This ensures that the judge assesses what the model says, rather than what it was asked to do. In RAG settings, responses that are not grounded in the retrieved context are flagged as hallucinations, while tool‑use outputs are validated against the set of allowed tools and their parameter schemas.</p>\n<p>We employ specialized judge prompts tailored to different SFT data types, including multi‑turn dialogue, RAG‑augmented responses, tool‑calling interactions, and multilingual conversations. Each response is scored across six weighted dimensions—instruction following, correctness, completeness, conciseness, naturalness, and calibration (with optional critical‑thinking checks). Samples are accepted, flagged as borderline, or rejected based on deterministic score thresholds, with hard‑reject rules overriding scores for severe defects such as hallucinations, false premises, or incorrect computations.</p>\n<p>To complement semantic evaluation, we apply a deterministic rule‑based pipeline that enforces structural integrity through text normalization, truncation and length filtering, schema validation, and leakage detection. A final global deduplication step ensures dataset‑wide uniqueness. All filtering and correction actions are fully auditable.</p>\n<h3>\n\t<a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://huggingface.co/blog/ibm-granite/granite-4-1#sft-training-details\">\n\t</a>\n\t<span>\n\t\tSFT Training Details\n\t</span>\n</h3>\n<p>After passing through the LLM-as-Judge, rule-based filtering, and global deduplication pipeline, we fine-tune base models on these approximately <strong>4.1 million</strong> high-quality samples. Rather than a single RL pass, we run <strong>multiple targeted RL stages</strong>, each optimizing for different capabilities.</p>\n<h3>\n\t<a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://huggingface.co/blog/ibm-granite/granite-4-1#training-methodology\">\n\t</a>\n\t<span>\n\t\tTraining Methodology\n\t</span>\n</h3>\n<p>We use <strong>On-policy GRPO (Group Relative Policy Optimization)</strong> (<a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.03300\">Shao et al., 2024</a>) with <strong>DAPO (Decoupled Clip and Dynamic sAmpling Policy Optimization) loss</strong> (<a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.14476\">Yu et al., 2025</a>) which provides more stable training signals compared to standard GRPO. With this stage, we observed an average improvement of <strong>~18.9 points</strong> (averaged across the three Granite 4.1 models) in Alpaca-Eval compared to the SFT checkpoints.</p>\n<p>To mitigate policy drift from its previously learned knowledge, we use a conservative learning rate of <code>3e-7</code> and higher KL-loss coeff $\\beta$ of <code>0.09</code> in this stage. We use an average of 17,920 unique prompts in this RLHF stage.</p>\n<h4>\n\t<a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://huggingface.co/blog/ibm-granite/granite-4-1#identity--knowledge-calibration-rl\">\n\t</a>\n\t<span>\n\t\tIdentity &amp; Knowledge-Calibration RL\n\t</span>\n</h4>\n<p>In this stage, we train the model for a few steps (~40 training steps) on identity and knowledge calibration prompts. We observed that this small training stage significantly improves the model's self-identification capabilities.</p>\n<p>Similar to the RLHF stage, we used a learning rate of <code>3e-7</code> and KL-loss coeff $\\beta$ of <code>0.09</code>, and we use 1728 unique prompts in this stage.</p>\n<h4>\n\t<a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://huggingface.co/blog/ibm-granite/granite-4-1#math-rl\">\n\t</a>\n\t<span>\n\t\tMath RL\n\t</span>\n</h4>\n<p>During our RL training, we found that the RLHF stage causes a drop in math benchmark scores (e.g., in GSM8K, DeepMind-Math).
