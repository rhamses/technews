Codon optimization determines <em>which DNA</em> will produce those amino acids efficiently in a living cell.</p>\n<div>\n\t<table>\n\t\t<thead><tr>\n<th>Component</th>\n<th>What We Did</th>\n<th>Key Result</th>\n</tr>\n\t\t</thead><tbody><tr>\n<td><strong>Protein Folding</strong></td>\n<td>ESMFold v1 predictions on 30 protein chains</td>\n<td>Avg PTM: 0.79, working batch pipeline</td>\n</tr>\n<tr>\n<td><strong>Sequence Design</strong></td>\n<td>ProteinMPNN on scaffold 7K00</td>\n<td>42% sequence recovery</td>\n</tr>\n<tr>\n<td><strong>mRNA Optimization</strong></td>\n<td>Trained multiple transformer variants on 250k CDS, then scaled to 381k sequences across 25 species</td>\n<td><strong>CodonRoBERTa-large-v2</strong>: perplexity 4.10, CAI 0.40; <strong>Multi-species suite</strong>: 4 models spanning 25 organisms (55 GPU-hours)</td>\n</tr>\n</tbody>\n\t</table>\n</div>\n<p>The mRNA optimization work is where we invested the most effort, and where we have the most to share. The codon optimization component is entirely ours: new models, new training infrastructure, new evaluation metrics.</p>\n<h2>\n\t<a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://huggingface.co/blog/OpenMed/training-mrna-models-25-species#2-the-architecture-exploration\">\n\t</a>\n\t<span>\n\t\t2. The Architecture Exploration\n\t</span>\n</h2>\n<p>In Part I, we surveyed the protein AI landscape and noted that most biological language models are adaptations of NLP architectures. The open question was <em>which</em> architecture. BERT variants dominate protein modeling (ESM-2, ProtTrans), but codon sequences have different statistical properties than both natural language and amino acid sequences. Codons are triplets drawn from a small 64-token alphabet, with strong positional dependencies and species-specific usage biases. We needed to find out what works from first principles.</p>\n<p>The core question: which transformer architecture works best for codon-level language modeling?</p>\n<p>This matters because codon optimization is crucial for therapeutic mRNA, vaccines, and recombinant protein production. The genetic code is degenerate: the same protein can be encoded by astronomically many different DNA sequences, but some codon arrangements express 100x better than others. The Pfizer-BioNTech COVID vaccine, for example, was codon-optimized for human expression. We wanted to build a model that could learn these preferences directly from natural coding sequences, rather than relying on hand-crafted frequency tables.</p>\n<h3>\n\t<a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://huggingface.co/blog/OpenMed/training-mrna-models-25-species#the-contenders\">\n\t</a>\n\t<span>\n\t\tThe Contenders\n\t</span>\n</h3>\n<p>We started with a small CodonBERT baseline (6M params, following Sanofi's published architecture) and scaled up through two families: ModernBERT, which represented the latest efficiency innovations from the NLP community, and RoBERTa, the proven workhorse behind Meta's ESM protein language models.</p>\n<div>\n\t<table>\n\t\t<thead><tr>\n<th>Model</th>\n<th>Parameters</th>\n<th>Architecture</th>\n<th>Hypothesis</th>\n</tr>\n\t\t</thead><tbody><tr>\n<td><strong>CodonBERT (baseline)</strong></td>\n<td>6M</td>\n<td>BERT-tiny (6 layers)</td>\n<td>Minimal baseline to establish floor performance</td>\n</tr>\n<tr>\n<td><strong>ModernBERT-base</strong></td>\n<td>90M</td>\n<td>ModernBERT (22 layers, RoPE)</td>\n<td>Modern innovations: long context, efficient attention</td>\n</tr>\n<tr>\n<td><strong>CodonRoBERTa-base</strong></td>\n<td>92M</td>\n<td>RoBERTa (12 layers)</td>\n<td>Proven MLM architecture, same family as ESM-2</td>\n</tr>\n<tr>\n<td><strong>CodonRoBERTa-large</strong></td>\n<td>312M</td>\n<td>RoBERTa (24 layers)</td>\n<td>Test whether more parameters improve codon modeling</td>\n</tr>\n<tr>\n<td><strong>CodonRoBERTa-large-v2</strong></td>\n<td>312M</td>\n<td>RoBERTa (24 layers, refined)</td>\n<td>Same architecture, better hyperparameters</td>\n</tr>\n</tbody>\n\t</table>\n</div>\n<p>The choice of RoBERTa was deliberate. As we discussed in Part I, Meta's ESM-2 (which powers ESMFold) is itself a RoBERTa variant trained on protein sequences. We hypothesized that the same architecture family that learned amino acid patterns might also learn codon patterns. ModernBERT was the counterpoint: a 2024 architecture with RoPE embeddings, Flash Attention, and alternating local/global attention layers, representing everything the NLP community had learned since RoBERTa's 2019 release.</p>\n<h3>\n\t<a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://huggingface.co/blog/OpenMed/training-mrna-models-25-species#the-training-setup\">\n\t</a>\n\t<span>\n\t\tThe Training Setup\n\t</span>\n</h3>\n<p>To ensure a fair comparison, every model was trained on identical data with the same evaluation protocol. We used 250,000 coding sequences (CDS) from <em>E. coli</em> RefSeq, covering chromosome and complete assembly accessions. This is a clean, well-annotated dataset where codon usage patterns are well-characterized in the literature, giving us ground truth to validate against.</p>\n<p>Our tokenizer maps each codon to a single token: 64 codons plus 5 special tokens (PAD, UNK, CLS, SEP, MASK) for a 69-token vocabulary. This is intentionally minimal. Unlike BPE tokenizers used in NLP, where subword boundaries are statistically learned, codon boundaries are biologically defined. Every three nucleotides encode one amino acid. Our tokenizer respects this.</p>\n<p>Training ran on 4 A100 GPUs (80GB) with FSDP sharding, using 15,000 to 25,000 steps depending on model size. All models used masked language modeling (MLM) with 15% masking rate, the same objective used by ESM-2 for protein sequences.</p>\n<h3>\n\t<a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://huggingface.co/blog/OpenMed/training-mrna-models-25-species#the-results\">\n\t</a>\n\t<span>\n\t\tThe Results\n\t</span>\n</h3>\n<div>\n\t<table>\n\t\t<thead><tr>\n<th>Model</th>\n<th>Perplexity</th>\n<th>CAI Spearman</th>\n<th>Synonymous Recovery</th>\n<th>Status</th>\n</tr>\n\t\t</thead><tbody><tr>\n<td><strong>CodonRoBERTa-large-v2</strong></td>\n<td>4.10</td>\n<td><strong>0.404</strong></td>\n<td>7.7%</td>\n<td><strong>Best Overall</strong></td>\n</tr>\n<tr>\n<td><strong>CodonRoBERTa-base</strong></td>\n<td><strong>4.01</strong></td>\n<td>0.219</td>\n<td><strong>8.5%</strong></td>\n<td><strong>Best Efficiency</strong></td>\n</tr>\n<tr>\n<td>CodonRoBERTa-large</td>\n<td>4.01</td>\n<td>0.025</td>\n<td>7.6%</td>\n<td>Good MLM, weak bio</td>\n</tr>\n<tr>\n<td>ModernBERT-base</td>\n<td>26.24</td>\n<td>0.070</td>\n<td>8.5%</td>\n<td>Underperformed</td>\n</tr>\n<tr>\n<td>CodonBERT (baseline)</td>\n<td>17.18</td>\n<td>-0.629</td>\n<td>0.0%</td>\n<td>Baseline</td>\n</tr>\n</tbody>\n\t</table>\n</div>\n<p>The result was unambiguous: RoBERTa outperformed ModernBERT by 6x on perplexity (4.01 vs 26.24). This was not a marginal difference. ModernBERT, despite its modern attention patterns and efficient architecture, fundamentally underperformed the classic RoBERTa design on codon sequences.</p>\n<h3>\n\t<a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://huggingface.co/blog/OpenMed/training-mrna-models-25-species#what-we-learned\">\n\t</a>\n\t<span>\n\t\tWhat We Learned\n\t</span>\n</h3>\n<p><strong>1. Pre-trained NLP weights do not transfer to biology</strong></p>\n<p>We initialized ModernBERT from its published English-language checkpoint, expecting the learned attention patterns to provide a useful starting point. They did not. Our best explanation: ModernBERT's pre-training on English text instilled inductive biases (subword frequency distributions, positional attention patterns) that actively interfere with learning codon statistics. RoBERTa, initialized randomly and trained purely on biological data, had no such baggage. This aligns with what the field has seen more broadly: ESM-2 and ProtTrans both train from scratch on biological data rather than fine-tuning from NLP checkpoints.</p>\n<p><strong>2. Hyperparameter tuning unlocked biological alignment</strong></p>\n<p>This was the most surprising and practically important finding of the exploration. Compare CodonRoBERTa-large v1 and v2:</p>\n<div>\n\t<table>\n\t\t<thead><tr>\n<th>Version</th>\n<th>Perplexity</th>\n<th>CAI Spearman</th>\n</tr>\n\t\t</thead><tbody><tr>\n<td>v1 (lr=1e-4)</td>\n<td>4.01</td>\n<td>0.025</td>\n</tr>\n<tr>\n<td>v2 (lr=5e-5, longer warmup)</td>\n<td>4.10</td>\n<td><strong>0.404</strong></td>\n</tr>\n</tbody>\n\t</table>\n</div>\n<p>Same architecture. Same data. Same number of parameters. The only differences: half the learning rate and a longer warmup (2,000 steps vs 1,000). Yet v2's predicted codon likelihoods are <strong>16x better correlated with real biological codon preferences</strong>, as measured by Codon Adaptation Index.</p>\n<p>The perplexity actually got slightly <em>worse</em> (4.10 vs 4.01), which means v2 is marginally less accurate at predicting the exact masked codon. But it is dramatically better at predicting codons that biology actually uses. The slower training schedule let the model settle into representations that capture genuine biological signal rather than overfitting to surface statistics.</p>\n<p>This is a crucial insight for anyone training biological language models: <strong>MLM loss alone does not measure biological relevance.</strong> Domain-specific metrics are essential. In our case, CAI correlation turned out to be the metric that separates a useful model from a technically impressive but biologically meaningless one.</p>\n<p><strong>3. The base model is remarkably efficient</strong></p>\n<p>CodonRoBERTa-base (92M params) achieved nearly identical perplexity to the large model (4.01 vs 4.10) with 3.4x fewer parameters and proportionally less training time. Its CAI correlation (0.219) is lower than v2's (0.404), but still well above the baseline and ModernBERT. For teams without access to multi-GPU clusters, the base model is a practical choice that captures most of the codon modeling performance at a fraction of the cost.</p>\n<h2>\n\t<a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://huggingface.co/blog/OpenMed/training-mrna-models-25-species#3-the-pipeline\">\n\t</a>\n\t<span>\n\t\t3. Here we run each stage with real data and report what we actually got.</p>\n<ol>\n<li><strong>Fold</strong>: Predict the 3D structure (ESMFold)</li>\n<li><strong>Design</strong>: Generate sequences that fold into that structure (ProteinMPNN)</li>\n<li><strong>Optimize</strong>: Choose the best codons for expression (CodonRoBERTa)</li>\n</ol>\n<hr>\n<h3>\n\t<a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://huggingface.co/blog/OpenMed/training-mrna-models-25-species#31-protein-folding-with-esmfold\">\n\t</a>\n\t<span>\n\t\t3.1 Protein Folding with ESMFold\n\t</span>\n</h3>\n<p><a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://cdn-uploads.huggingface.co/production/uploads/5fd5e18a90b6dc4633f6d292/ty2O24OhgfrFHJJUTl7dG.jpeg\"><img src=\"https://cdn-uploads.huggingface.co/production/uploads/5fd5e18a90b6dc4633f6d292/ty2O24OhgfrFHJJUTl7dG.jpeg\" alt=\"ESMFold Architecture: single-sequence structure prediction using the ESM-2 protein language model\"></a></p>\n<p><em>ESMFold architecture. Figure from Bertoline et al., Biomolecules 2024, CC-BY 4.0.</em></p>\n<p>As covered in Part I, ESMFold is Meta's single-sequence structure predictor. It uses ESM-2, a 15-billion-parameter protein language model trained on 65 million UniRef sequences, as its backbone. The key advantage over AlphaFold 2 is speed: ESMFold skips the computationally expensive multiple sequence alignment (MSA) step and predicts structures directly from a single amino acid sequence. (C) Tied positions enable symmetric and multi-state design. Figure from Dauparas et al., Science 2022, CC-BY 4.0.</em></p>\n<p>As we described in Part I, protein design is the inverse of protein folding. Folding goes sequence to structure: given amino acids, predict the 3D shape. <strong>Inverse folding</strong> goes the other way: given a target 3D shape, find amino acid sequences that will fold into it.</p>\n<p>ProteinMPNN, from David Baker's lab at the University of Washington, is the current gold standard for this task. It was published in <em>Science</em> in 2022 and has since been validated experimentally: designed sequences fold into their target structures at rates far exceeding random or earlier computational methods. The architecture treats the protein backbone as a graph, where nodes are amino acid positions and edges connect spatially proximate residues (K-nearest neighbors in 3D). A message-passing neural network propagates information through this graph, then autoregressively generates a sequence one residue at a time.</p>\n<h3>\n\t<a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://huggingface.co/blog/OpenMed/training-mrna-models-25-species#our-results-scaffold-7k00\">\n\t</a>\n\t<span>\n\t\tOur Results: Scaffold 7K00\n\t</span>\n</h3>\n<p>We ran ProteinMPNN on PDB structure 7K00 (a large multi-chain ribosomal complex):</p>\n<pre><code>python proteinmpnn/protein_mpnn_run.py \\\n    --pdb_path data/pdb/raw/7K00.cif \\\n    --out_folder outputs/proteinmpnn_smoke \\\n    --num_seq_per_target 3 \\\n    --sampling_temp 0.1\n</code></pre>\n<p><strong>Results:</strong></p>\n<div>\n\t<table>\n\t\t<thead><tr>\n<th>Metric</th>\n<th>Value</th>\n</tr>\n\t\t</thead><tbody><tr>\n<td>Sequences generated</td>\n<td>3</td>\n</tr>\n<tr>\n<td>Best score</td>\n<td>0.89</td>\n</tr>\n<tr>\n<td>Sequence recovery</td>\n<td>~42%</td>\n</tr>\n</tbody>\n\t</table>\n</div>\n<p>Here's what the output looks like:</p>\n<pre><code>&gt;7K00, score=1.7100, global_score=1.7100\nGIREKIKLVSSAGTGHFYTTTKNKRTKPEKLELKKFDPVVRQHVIYKEAKI/MKRTFQPSVLK...\n&gt;T=0.1, sample=1, score=0.8857, seq_recovery=0.4203\nSKKVVIKLVCSCGCGFEYCDFRDIEKNPEKIERVLYCPICQKYVLFTEAPL/PPGPFRPDREV...\n</code></pre>\n<p>The first line is the native (natural) sequence extracted from the crystal structure. Subsequent lines are ProteinMPNN's designed variants. At temperature 0.1 (low randomness), the model recovers ~42% of the original amino acids, purely from 3D geometry. This is a strong result: it means the model independently rediscovered nearly half the residues that evolution selected, using only the backbone coordinates as input.</p>\n<p>Several practical notes from running ProteinMPNN. Scores are negative log-likelihoods, so lower is better. The 42% recovery rate is typical for well-resolved structures and consistent with the original paper's benchmarks. Higher sampling temperatures produce more diverse but riskier sequences. For real design work, the most powerful feature is <strong>partial design</strong>: catalytic residues, binding site amino acids, or any positions with known functional importance can be fixed in place, while ProteinMPNN redesigns only the scaffold around them. This is the standard approach for engineering a more stable version of an enzyme without disrupting its active site.</p>\n<hr>\n<h3>\n\t<a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://huggingface.co/blog/OpenMed/training-mrna-models-25-species#33-mrna-optimization\">\n\t</a>\n\t<span>\n\t\t3.3 mRNA Optimization\n\t</span>\n</h3>\n<p>This is where the pipeline transitions from existing tools to our own models. ESMFold and ProteinMPNN are established, well-validated software that we integrated. Codon optimization is where we built something new.</p>\n<h4>\n\t<a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://huggingface.co/blog/OpenMed/training-mrna-models-25-species#why-codon-choice-matters\">\n\t</a>\n\t<span>\n\t\tWhy Codon Choice Matters\n\t</span>\n</h4>\n<p><a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://cdn-uploads.huggingface.co/production/uploads/5fd5e18a90b6dc4633f6d292/pJIUjRAYERh0YBo6wlxMV.png\"><img src=\"https://cdn-uploads.huggingface.co/production/uploads/5fd5e18a90b6dc4633f6d292/pJIUjRAYERh0YBo6wlxMV.png\" alt=\"Codon usage frequency heatmaps across optimization tools for E. coli, S. cerevisiae, and CHO cells\"></a>\n<em>Codon usage frequencies vary dramatically between organisms. These heatmaps compare codon preferences across E. coli, yeast, and CHO cells, the three expression hosts covered by our multi-species models. Figure from Kim et al., J. Microbiol. Biotechnol. 2025, CC-BY 4.0.</em></p>\n<p>The genetic code is <strong>degenerate</strong>: most amino acids are encoded by multiple codons. Leucine, for example, has six: TTA, TTG, CTT, CTC, CTA, and CTG. All six produce the same amino acid in the final protein. Methionine and tryptophan are the exceptions, with only one codon each.</p>\n<p>This redundancy means that for any given protein, there are astronomically many DNA sequences that encode it. A typical 300-amino-acid protein has roughly 10^150 possible codon combinations. They all produce the same amino acid chain, but they do <em>not</em> all produce the same amount of protein. Codon choice affects translation speed (because tRNA molecules are not equally abundant for all codons), mRNA stability (because the nucleotide sequence affects how quickly the transcript degrades), co-translational folding (because translation pauses at rare codons give the protein time to fold), and immune recognition (because the innate immune system in mammalian cells can distinguish native from foreign mRNA patterns). In practice, bad codon choices can reduce protein expression by 100x. This is why every mRNA vaccine, every recombinant protein therapeutic, and every gene therapy vector goes through codon optimization.</p>\n<h4>\n\t<a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://huggingface.co/blog/OpenMed/training-mrna-models-25-species#the-traditional-approach-and-why-it-is-limited\">\n\t</a>\n\t<span>\n\t\tThe Traditional Approach and Why It Is Limited\n\t</span>\n</h4>\n<p><a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://cdn-uploads.huggingface.co/production/uploads/5fd5e18a90b6dc4633f6d292/c5O20MNTQJluPtrzScAlO.png\"><img src=\"https://cdn-uploads.huggingface.co/production/uploads/5fd5e18a90b6dc4633f6d292/c5O20MNTQJluPtrzScAlO.png\" alt=\"The mRNA codon optimization design space: billions of possible sequences encoding the same protein\"></a></p>\n<p><em>The scale of the codon optimization problem. For a typical mRNA, there are over 10^600 possible codon sequences encoding the same protein. The challenge is finding the arrangement that maximizes expression. Figure from Zhang et al. (LinearDesign), Nature 2023, CC-BY 4.0.</em></p>\n<p>The classical method is simple: measure which codons appear most frequently in highly-expressed genes of the target organism, then replace every codon with the most frequent synonym. This is codified as the <strong>Codon Adaptation Index (CAI)</strong>, a per-sequence score that measures how closely the codon usage matches the organism's preferred distribution.</p>\n<p>CAI-based optimization works, but it is crude. It treats each codon position independently, ignoring the sequence context. It produces repetitive sequences (the same \"optimal\" codon used everywhere for a given amino acid), which can cause ribosome stalling and mRNA secondary structure problems. And it misses complex dependencies: the optimal codon at position 50 might depend on what codons are at positions 48 and 52, which a frequency table cannot capture.</p>\n<h4>\n\t<a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://huggingface.co/blog/OpenMed/training-mrna-models-25-species#our-approach-masked-language-modeling\">\n\t</a>\n\t<span>\n\t\tOur Approach: Masked Language Modeling\n\t</span>\n</h4>\n<p>We reframe codon optimization as a language modeling problem. Instead of looking up frequencies in a table, we train a transformer on hundreds of thousands of natural coding sequences using masked language modeling (MLM), the same pre-training objective used by BERT, RoBERTa, and Meta's ESM protein models. The model sees a codon sequence with 15% of positions masked and learns to predict the missing codons from context.</p>\n<p>What the model learns, implicitly, is the <em>grammar</em> of codon usage: which codon patterns appear in nature, which codons tend to co-occur, and how preferences shift depending on the surrounding sequence context. As we learned from the v1/v2 comparison above, a model can have excellent perplexity (accurately predicting masked codons) while having poor biological alignment (predicting codons that nature does not actually prefer). We compute the Codon Adaptation Index for each test sequence, then correlate it with the model's pseudo-log-likelihood score. A positive correlation means the model assigns higher probability to sequences that biology actually uses. This is the metric that matters most for practical codon optimization, because it directly measures whether the model has learned biologically relevant patterns vs. just statistical ones. The End-to-End Workflow\n\t</span>\n</h2>\n<p><a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://cdn-uploads.huggingface.co/production/uploads/5fd5e18a90b6dc4633f6d292/ZAHq19e94YYQ0ixlnPg5I.jpeg\"><img src=\"https://cdn-uploads.huggingface.co/production/uploads/5fd5e18a90b6dc4633f6d292/ZAHq19e94YYQ0ixlnPg5I.jpeg\" alt=\"The two-step protein design paradigm: generate backbone structure, then optimize sequence\"></a></p>\n<p><em>The modern computational protein design workflow. Structure generation (top) produces backbone coordinates; sequence optimization (bottom) finds amino acid sequences that fold into the target shape. Our pipeline adds a third step: codon optimization for expression. Figure from Kortemme, Cell 2024, CC-BY 4.0.</em></p>\n<p>In Part I, we described the protein engineering loop as a cycle of prediction, design, and optimization. Here is what that looks like in practice with <strong>OpenMed's</strong> pipeline. Each step feeds into the next, and the entire computational phase runs in an afternoon on a single GPU.</p>\n<p>Consider a concrete scenario: engineering a more stable version of a therapeutic enzyme that degrades too quickly in the bloodstream.</p>\n<p><strong>Step 1: Fold (ESMFold).</strong> Predict the structure of the starting sequence to understand its active site and identify regions that might be destabilized. ESMFold returns a 3D structure in PDB format, per-residue confidence scores (pLDDT) highlighting uncertain regions, and an overall topology confidence metric (PTM).</p>\n<p><strong>Step 2: Design (ProteinMPNN).</strong> Keep the active site fixed but redesign the scaffold for improved stability. ProteinMPNN takes the backbone coordinates, a list of immutable positions (catalytic residues), and generates 100 diverse candidate sequences, each predicted to fold into the target shape.</p>\n<p><strong>Step 3: Verify (ESMFold).</strong> Refold all 100 candidates with ESMFold to confirm they still adopt the correct shape. Filter for high mean pLDDT (&gt;80), correct topology (RMSD to original), and low clash scores.</p>\n<p><strong>Step 4: Optimize (CodonRoBERTa).</strong> Take the best amino acid sequences and use CodonRoBERTa-large-v2 to generate optimal DNA sequences for expression in the target organism (<em>E. coli</em>, yeast, or mammalian cells). The model scores synonymous codon choices based on learned biological preferences, identifies contextually optimal codons rather than just globally frequent ones, and produces sequences with high CAI correlation.</p>\n<p><strong>Step 5: Synthesize and Test.</strong> Order the DNA from a synthesis company, clone it into an expression vector, and test expression and activity in the lab.</p>\n<p>This loop, from hypothesis to synthesis-ready DNA, replaces what once took months of iterative wet-lab trial and error. A researcher arrives at the bench with 5-10 computationally vetted candidates instead of one or two educated guesses. The success rate improves, the cost drops, and the design cycle compresses from months to days.</p>\n<hr>\n<p>For a full ecosystem overview, tool selection guide, and license reference, see Part I.</p>\n<hr>\n<h2>\n\t<a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://huggingface.co/blog/OpenMed/training-mrna-models-25-species#6-where-this-stands-and-whats-next\">\n\t</a>\n\t<span>\n\t\t6. Two recent models push the codon/mRNA modeling frontier further:</p>\n<ul>\n<li><p><strong>mRNABERT</strong> (Xiong et al., <em>Nature Communications</em> 2025): 86M-param BERT with a dual tokenization scheme (single nucleotides for UTRs, codons for CDS) and cross-modality contrastive learning against frozen ProtT5-XL protein embeddings. Trained on 115M sequences across ~25,000 species. Beats CodonBERT on 11/13 BEACON benchmark tasks. No code or weights released.</p>\n</li>\n</ul>\n<p>Both models train on 50-300x more data than <strong>OpenMed</strong> used. That is the primary gap, and we are transparent about it.</p>\n<p>Here is what <strong>OpenMed</strong> does that neither of them offers:</p>\n<ol>\n<li><p><strong>Species-conditioned single model.</strong> mRNABERT has no species conditioning at all. NUWA trains three separate models (one per domain of life). We put 25 species tokens into one 94-token vocabulary and train a single model that can be prompted for any organism. More parameter-efficient, more flexible.</p>\n</li>\n<li><p><strong>Transfer learning to low-resource organisms, validated.</strong> We showed that fine-tuning the multispecies base on just 8.5k <em>E. coli</em> sequences improves over the base model. Neither mRNABERT nor NUWA demonstrates this.</p>\n</li>\n<li><p><strong>Full open-source pipeline.</strong> ESMFold + ProteinMPNN + CodonRoBERTa, end to end, with training code, configs, eval scripts, and model weights. All Apache 2.0. mRNABERT releases code but not the pipeline. NUWA releases nothing.</p>\n</li>\n</ol>\n<h3>\n\t<a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://huggingface.co/blog/OpenMed/training-mrna-models-25-species#in-progress-codonjepa\">\n\t</a>\n\t<span>\n\t\tIn Progress: CodonJEPA\n\t</span>\n</h3>\n<p><strong>OpenMed</strong> is running a proof-of-concept for a fundamentally different approach: <strong>Joint Embedding Predictive Architecture (JEPA) for codon sequences</strong>.</p>\n<p>Standard MLM predicts masked <em>tokens</em>. JEPA predicts masked <em>embeddings</em>. The hypothesis: if the model is forced to predict in embedding space rather than token space, it should learn that synonymous codons (different DNA, same amino acid) are functionally equivalent. MLM cannot achieve this because it is trained to distinguish every token.</p>\n<p>The architecture:</p>\n<ul>\n<li><strong>Context encoder</strong>: RoBERTa-base (768-dim, 12 layers), trained normally</li>\n<li><strong>Target encoder</strong>: EMA copy of the context encoder (momentum 0.990 to 0.999, no gradients)</li>\n<li><strong>Predictor</strong>: Lightweight 4-layer transformer (384-dim), predicts target embeddings from context</li>\n<li><strong>Masking</strong>: Multi-block strategy (4 contiguous blocks per sequence, 15-20% mask scale)</li>\n<li><strong>Collapse prevention</strong>: VICReg regularization (variance + covariance loss)</li>\n</ul>\n<p>Early results from our evaluation suite (JEPA vs. MLM baseline, same data, same hyperparameters, 15k steps each):</p>\n<div>\n\t<table>\n\t\t<thead><tr>\n<th>Metric</th>\n<th>CodonJEPA</th>\n<th>CodonMLM</th>\n<th>Winner</th>\n</tr>\n\t\t</thead><tbody><tr>\n<td>Synonymous robustness (cosine sim)</td>\n<td><strong>0.9997</strong></td>\n<td>0.9414</td>\n<td>JEPA</td>\n</tr>\n<tr>\n<td>Codon-to-amino-acid probe</td>\n<td>37.4%</td>\n<td>100%</td>\n<td>MLM (expected)</td>\n</tr>\n<tr>\n<td>Taxonomy classification</td>\n<td>87.8%</td>\n<td>99.4%</td>\n<td>MLM</td>\n</tr>\n<tr>\n<td>Effective dimensionality</td>\n<td>1.67</td>\n<td>18.71</td>\n<td>MLM</td>\n</tr>\n</tbody>\n\t</table>\n</div>\n<p>The synonymous robustness result is the one that matters most for our hypothesis. JEPA embeddings are nearly identical (99.97% cosine similarity) for sequences that differ only in synonymous codon choices. MLM embeddings shift significantly (94.14%). This means JEPA <em>does</em> learn that synonymous codons are interchangeable, exactly as predicted.</p>\n<p>The open challenge: JEPA currently suffers from dimensional collapse (91.78% of variance concentrated in one component). This is a known failure mode for self-supervised methods and suggests the VICReg regularization weights need tuning. The architecture works; the training dynamics need more iteration.</p>\n<p>This is early-stage research, not production-ready. But if the collapse problem can be solved, JEPA could produce codon embeddings that are inherently amino-acid-aware, something that MLM, by its token-level prediction objective, fundamentally cannot achieve.</p>\n<h3>\n\t<a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://huggingface.co/blog/OpenMed/training-mrna-models-25-species#roadmap\">\n\t</a>\n\t<span>\n\t\tRoadmap\n\t</span>\n</h3>\n<p><strong>CodonRoBERTa (scaling up)</strong>:</p>\n<ul>\n<li>Retrain on mRNABERT's public 36M-sequence dataset (Zenodo).
