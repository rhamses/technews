href=\"https://huggingface.co/blog/Photoroom/prx-part3#introduction\">\n\t</a>\n\t<span>\n\t\tIntroduction\n\t</span>\n</h2>\n<p>Welcome back 👋</p>\n<p>In the last two posts (<a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://huggingface.co/blog/Photoroom/prx-part1-architectures\">Part 1</a> and <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://huggingface.co/blog/Photoroom/prx-part2\">Part 2</a>), we explored a wide range of architectural and training tricks for diffusion models. We tried to evaluate each idea in isolation, measuring throughput, convergence speed, and final image quality, and tried to understand what actually moves the needle.</p>\n<p>In this post, we want to answer a much more practical question:</p>\n<p><strong>What happens when we combine all the tricks that worked?</strong></p>\n<p>Instead of optimizing one dimension at a time, we’ll stack the most promising ingredients together and see how far we can push performance under a strict compute budget.</p>\n<p>To make things concrete, we’re doing a <strong>24-hour speedrun</strong>:</p>\n<ul>\n<li><strong>32 H200</strong></li>\n<li>~<strong>$1500 total compute budget (2$/hour/GPU)</strong></li>\n</ul>\n<p>This is very far from the early diffusion days, where training competitive models could cost millions of dollars. The goal here is to demonstrate how much the field has evolved and how far careful engineering can take you in just a single day of training.</p>\n<p>This speedrun is not just a fun experiment. It will likely serve as the foundation for our large-scale training recipe going forward.</p>\n<p>Alongside the results, we’re also open-sourcing our code (<a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://github.com/Photoroom/PRX\">Github link</a>), which contains:</p>\n<ul>\n<li>The training code used for this speedrun</li>\n<li>The experimental framework from the previous blog post</li>\n</ul>\n<p>So you can reproduce, modify, and extend everything yourself.</p>\n<hr>\n<h2>\n\t<a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://huggingface.co/blog/Photoroom/prx-part3#the-training-recipe\">\n\t</a>\n\t<span>\n\t\tThe Training Recipe\n\t</span>\n</h2>\n<p>Now let’s walk through what went into this 24h run.</p>\n<h3>\n\t<a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://huggingface.co/blog/Photoroom/prx-part3#x-prediction-and-training-in-the-pixel-space\">\n\t</a>\n\t<span>\n\t\tX-prediction and Training in the Pixel Space\n\t</span>\n</h3>\n<p>We use the x-prediction formulation from <em>Back to Basics: Let Denoising Generative Models Denoise</em> [<a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.13720\">Li and He, 2025</a>]. As seen in <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://huggingface.co/blog/Photoroom/prx-part2\">Part 2</a>, this enables <strong>training directly in pixel space and eliminates the need for a VAE altogether</strong>.\nWe use a patch size of 32 and use a 256-dimensional bottleneck in the initial token projection layer. This design keeps the sequence length under control, making pixel-space training computationally manageable even at higher resolutions.</p>\n<p>At 512px, the sequence length is:</p>\n<p><span><span><span></span></span></span></p>\n<p>At 1024px, the sequence length becomes:</p>\n<p><span><span><span></span></span></span></p>\n<p>Instead of following the usual 256px → 512px → 1024px schedule, we start directly at 512px and then fine-tune at 1024px. </p>\n<p>With controlled token counts and modern hardware, pixel-space training is no longer prohibitive. It is simply a cleaner and more direct formulation.</p>\n<hr>\n<h3>\n\t<a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://huggingface.co/blog/Photoroom/prx-part3#perceptual-losses\">\n\t</a>\n\t<span>\n\t\tPerceptual Losses\n\t</span>\n</h3>\n<p>One very nice side effect of predicting <span><span></span></span> directly in pixel space is that we can reuse a whole toolbox from classical computer vision.</p>\n<p>When your model outputs latents, perceptual supervision becomes awkward. You either have to decode back to pixels or define losses in a learned latent space that may or may not align with human perception. Once you predict pixels directly, everything becomes straightforward again. You can plug in perceptual losses exactly as they were originally designed.</p>\n<p>We take inspiration from the paper <em>PixelGen: Pixel Diffusion Beats Latent Diffusion with Perceptual Loss</em> [<a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.02493\">Ma et al.</a>], where the authors introduce additional perceptual objectives on top of the diffusion loss. They show that adding perceptual signals can noticeably improve convergence speed and final visual quality.</p>\n<p>For this 24h run, we add two auxiliary losses:</p>\n<ul>\n<li><strong>LPIPS</strong> ([<a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://arxiv.org/abs/1801.03924\">Zhang et al.</a>])</li>\n<li>A <strong>DINO-based perceptual loss</strong> (we use DINOv2 [<a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.07193\">Oquab et al.</a>])</li>\n</ul>\n<p>The idea is simple: In addition to the standard flow matching objective, we encourage the predicted clean image to match the target image in a perceptual feature space. LPIPS captures low-level perceptual similarity, while DINO features provide a stronger semantic signal.</p>\n<p>We keep the same overall idea as the paper, but we tweaked a few details. In our experiments, we empirically found that it worked better to:</p>\n<ul>\n<li>apply the perceptual losses on pooled full images instead of patch-wise features</li>\n<li>apply them at all noise levels</li>\n</ul>\n<p>These are small implementation details, but in our setting they consistently gave better results.</p>\n<p>We used a weight of <strong>0.1</strong> for the LPIPS loss and <strong>0.01</strong> for the DINO perceptual loss, matching the values recommended in the original paper.</p>\n<p>These losses are lightweight compared to the main transformer forward pass, and in our setup they add only a small overhead while providing a consistent quality boost.</p>\n<h3>\n\t<a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://huggingface.co/blog/Photoroom/prx-part3#token-routing-with-tread\">\n\t</a>\n\t<span>\n\t\tToken Routing with TREAD\n\t</span>\n</h3>\n<p>To make each step cheaper, we use token routing with <em>TREAD</em> [<a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://openaccess.thecvf.com/content/ICCV2025/papers/Krause_TREAD_Token_Routing_for_Efficient_Architecture-agnostic_Diffusion_Training_ICCV_2025_paper.pdf\">Krause et al., 2025</a>]), which randomly selects a fraction of tokens and lets them bypass a contiguous chunk of transformer blocks, then re-injects them later so nothing is dropped. </p>\n<p>We picked TREAD over SPRINT (<a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.21986\">Park et al., 2025</a>) mostly for simplicity, and because the extra complexity of SPRINT did not feel worth the fairly small additional compute savings in our setting (sequence length 64 vs. 128 with TREAD at 512px).</p>\n<p>Following the TREAD recipe, we route <strong>50% of the tokens</strong> from the <strong>2nd block</strong> to the <strong>penultimate block</strong> of the transformer.</p>\n<p>Routed models can look worse under vanilla CFG, especially when undertrained, so we implemented a simple self-guidance scheme inspired by Guiding Token-Sparse Diffusion Models (<a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.01608\">Krause et al., 2025</a>), which guides using a dense vs. routed conditional prediction instead of relying on an unconditional branch. </p>\n<h3>\n\t<a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://huggingface.co/blog/Photoroom/prx-part3#representation-alignment-with-repa-and-dinov3\">\n\t</a>\n\t<span>\n\t\tRepresentation Alignment with REPA and DINOv3\n\t</span>\n</h3>\n<p>We used <strong>REPA</strong> [<a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.06940\">Yu et al., 2024</a>] for representation alignment.</p>\n<p>For the teacher, we went with <strong>DINOv3</strong> [<a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://arxiv.org/abs/2508.10104\">Siméoni et al. 2025</a>] since it gave the best quality improvements in our previous experiments.</p>\n<p>Concretely, we apply the alignment loss once,at the <strong>8th transformer block</strong> with a loss weight of <strong>0.5</strong>.</p>\n<p>Since we combine <strong>REPA</strong> with <strong>TREAD</strong> routing, we only compute the alignment loss on the <strong>non-routed tokens</strong>, meaning the tokens that actually go through the blocks where we apply the loss. This keeps the REPA signal consistent and avoids comparing features for tokens that skipped the computation path.</p>\n<hr>\n<h3>\n\t<a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://huggingface.co/blog/Photoroom/prx-part3#optimizer-muon\">\n\t</a>\n\t<span>\n\t\tOptimizer: Muon\n\t</span>\n</h3>\n<p>We used the <strong>Muon</strong> optimizer, using the FSDP implementation from <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://github.com/samsja/muon_fsdp_2\">muon_fsdp_2</a>, since it showed a clear improvement over Adam in our previous runs.</p>\n<p>Muon is only applied to <strong>2D parameters</strong> (basically matrices). Everything else (biases, norms, embeddings, etc.) is optimized with Adam, which is why the config has two parameter groups.</p>\n<div>\n\t<table>\n\t\t<thead><tr>\n<th>Group</th>\n<th>What it applies <code>eps=1e-8</code></td>\n</tr>\n</tbody>\n\t</table>\n</div>\n<h2>\n\t<a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://huggingface.co/blog/Photoroom/prx-part3#training-settings\">\n\t</a>\n\t<span>\n\t\tTraining Settings\n\t</span>\n</h2>\n<p>We trained on three publicly available synthetic datasets:</p>\n<ul>\n<li><strong>Flux generated</strong> (1.7M), <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://huggingface.co/datasets/lehduong/flux_generated\">lehduong/flux_generated</a>  </li>\n<li><strong>FLUX-Reason-6M</strong> (6M), <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://huggingface.co/datasets/LucasFang/FLUX-Reason-6M\">LucasFang/FLUX-Reason-6M</a>  </li>\n<li><strong>midjourney-v6-llava</strong> (1M), <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://huggingface.co/datasets/brivangl/midjourney-v6-llava\">brivangl/midjourney-v6-llava</a> which we re-captioned with <strong>Gemini 1.5</strong> to make prompts more consistent and cut down caption noise.</li>\n</ul>\n<p>The schedule is basically: go fast at 512, then sharpen at 1024:</p>\n<ul>\n<li><strong>512px</strong> for <strong>100k steps</strong> with <strong>batch size 1024</strong></li>\n<li><strong>1024px</strong> for <strong>20k steps</strong> with <strong>batch size 512</strong> without <strong>REPA</strong>.</li>\n</ul>\n<p>We also keep an <strong>EMA</strong> of the weights for sampling and eval:</p>\n<ul>\n<li><code>smoothing = 0.999</code></li>\n<li><code>update_interval = 10ba</code></li>\n<li><code>ema_start = 0ba</code></li>\n</ul>\n<h2>\n\t<a target=\"_blank\"
