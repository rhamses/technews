contentHtml: "<div><h2><strong></strong></h2><p>A production <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://redis.io/blog/what-is-an-ai-agent/\">AI agent</a> depends heavily on the context layer that tells it what to know at the moment it acts. It can pass every staging test, answer questions, call the right tools, and demo beautifully, then hit production and confidently offer a renewal discount to a customer who churned last quarter. The model was fine. The context it was fed was three months stale.</p><p>This story is playing out everywhere: <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2025-06-25-gartner-predicts-over-40-percent-of-agentic-ai-projects-will-be-canceled-by-end-of-2027\">Over 40%</a> of agentic AI projects are projected to be canceled by the end of 2027 due to escalating costs, unclear business value, and inadequate risk controls. In the postmortems I've read, the trail keeps leading back to that context layer. Prompt-level failures like context poisoning, distraction, confusion, clash, and context rot get most of the attention, and they matter. But they describe what goes wrong inside a context window. Four other failure modes describe what goes wrong in the infrastructure that fills it: fragmentation, opacity, speed degradation, and non-accumulation.</p><p>Each one has a fix, and the four fixes are the pillars of a production-ready context engine. This article covers what each failure mode looks like, why it tends to hide until production, and how a real-time context layer helps address all four.</p><h2><strong>1. Fragmentation: confident answers from a partial view</strong></h2><p>Start with fragmentation, because the other three inherit it. Enterprise context doesn't live in one place. Customer records sit in Salesforce, analytics tables in Snowflake, documents in Amazon Simple Storage Service (S3), and the rest is scattered across internal operational systems, document stores, <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://redis.io/resources/architecture-diagrams/redis-streams/\">event streams</a>, and app state. In many enterprises, no single system holds a complete or current picture of any given entity, and that's not an accident. These systems were adopted at different times, for different purposes, by different teams, and they were designed to interoperate with humans, not agents. A support rep can pivot across six browser tabs and mentally reconcile what they see. An agent typically can't unless you build that orchestration explicitly, and the groundwork for it is often missing. In a 2024 Gartner survey of 248 data management leaders, <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://www.gartner.com/en/newsroom/press-releases/2025-02-26-lack-of-ai-ready-data-puts-ai-projects-at-risk\">63% said</a> they either didn't have the right data management practices for AI or weren't sure they did.</p><h3><strong>The failure signature: confident &amp; wrong</strong></h3><p>What makes fragmentation dangerous is its failure signature. Agents rarely return errors when their view is partial; they return answers that are confident and internally coherent, built on a fraction of reality. Models tend to answer rather than abstain when their context is insufficient, and the stronger the model, the more polished the wrong answer sounds. The FinanceBench evaluation shows how much of that comes down to retrieval rather than reasoning: GPT-4-Turbo answered <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://docs.patronus.ai/docs/research_and_differentiators/financebench\">just 19% of questions</a> correctly when it searched one shared vector store covering every filing, but 85% when handed the right evidence page. Same model, same questions. The difference was the context it was given.</p><div><p><img alt=\"Redis Iris\" src=\"https://cdn.sanity.io/images/sy1jschh/production/82df1fa7f210a2b005d5ebbf3429a53a0ebccce0-64x64.svg\"></p><div><h2>Is your context production-ready?</h2><p><span>Score the retrieval, memory, and data layer behind your agents, and find the gaps before they reach production.</span></p></div></div><p>The risk is sharpest in operational contexts, because the gap goes unnoticed until a downstream action exposes it. An agent in a fragmented environment can't tell which tables to trust or whether a pipeline is flaky, so it queries stale or deprecated assets and produces answers that are systematically wrong.</p><h3><strong>The fix: fresh context</strong></h3><p>The fix for fragmentation is fresh context: one current picture of each entity, synced from the systems of record instead of assembled per query. Batch ETL can't get there, because anything pulled on a nightly or hourly schedule is already out of date when an agent reads it. Change data capture is the mechanism that can. <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://redis.io/data-integration/\">Redis Data Integration</a> keeps Redis in sync with existing relational databases, including Oracle, MySQL, PostgreSQL, and SQL Server, performing an initial sync and then capturing changes as they happen so updates land in Redis within seconds.</p><h2><strong>2. Opacity: the data exists, but agents can't use it</strong></h2><p>Even when the data exists and is reasonably current, agents often can't find their way through it. A semantic retrieval pipeline can pass an agent chunks of text ranked by similarity to its query, which works for simple lookups but can break down on the queries enterprise agents actually get:</p><ul><li><strong>Relationship traversal:</strong> enterprise questions tend to span linked records, and flat chunks can't carry the links. A supplier-to-component-to-product chain arrives as three unconnected fragments, leaving the agent nothing to walk. In one benchmark, chunk-based retrieval <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://arxiv.org/html/2606.06003v1\">broke down systematically</a> on queries that required reasoning across connected entities.</li><li><strong>Disambiguation:</strong> records that look alike but mean different things are hard to separate, and rare entities are the worst case. On templates as simple as \"where was [entity] born?\", keyword ranking (BM25) beat dense retrieval by <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://aclanthology.org/2021.emnlp-main.496.pdf\">49.8% on top-20 accuracy</a>. The nearest match in vector space isn't always the right record.</li><li><strong>Relevance vs. similarity:</strong> similar isn't the same as relevant. An embedding can rank a document that sits close to the query above the one that actually answers it, and exact terms like company names or metric labels, <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://arxiv.org/html/2604.01733v1\">strong retrieval signals</a> on their own, get blurred into the same space as everything else.</li></ul><h3><strong>The second face: access control</strong></h3><p>Give an agent raw <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://redis.io/glossary/databases/\">database credentials</a> or an unfiltered API, and controlling what it can see is very hard to get right. Text-to-SQL looks like a shortcut, but state-of-the-art agentic text-to-SQL scored just <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://arxiv.org/html/2409.02038v3\">10.8% accuracy</a> on private enterprise data in one benchmark. Security is another weak point: in another benchmark, injecting only <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://arxiv.org/html/2503.05445v1\">0.44% poisoned data</a> into training produced successful attacks, reaching a 79.41% attack success rate. The broader pattern has a name, <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://owasp.org/www-project-top-10-for-large-language-model-applications/assets/PDF/OWASP-Top-10-for-LLMs-v2025.pdf\">excessive agency</a>: agents with more functionality, permissions, and autonomy than the task needs.</p><h3><strong>The fix: navigable context</strong></h3><p>A semantic layer helps address both faces by making access control a first-class property of the architecture rather than something bolted onto query outputs. That's navigable context: a modeled path through the business rather than a set of credentials.</p><p>This is where Redis fits. <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://redis.io/iris/\">Redis Iris</a> is a real-time context engine that runs on Redis, backed by <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://redis.io/blog/database-performance-optimization-guide/\">sub-millisecond latency</a> for many core operations. <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://redis.io/docs/latest/develop/ai/context-engine/context-retriever/\">Redis Context Retriever</a> is the navigable piece: you define your data model once, specifying the entities that matter and the fields agents need, and it generates the governed tools agents call at runtime. Agents never touch the database directly, each one needs a key, and access tags filter what it can see. Underneath, <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://redis.io/blog/redis-8-4-open-source-ga/\">Redis Search</a> supports vector search, full-text search, and <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://university.redis.io/course/1npvvtfft2agew\">hybrid search</a>, where hybrid search combines full-text/keyword retrieval with vector similarity and optional filters on numeric, tag, text, or geospatial fields.</p><div><p><img alt=\"Redis Vector Database\" src=\"https://cdn.sanity.io/images/sy1jschh/production/ba774ea6ad5d6bf132ab3ef194b6a1ad7d7c914f-64x64.svg\"></p><div><h2>Grade your context layer</h2><p><span>Most teams are further behind on context infrastructure than they think. Score yours.</span></p></div></div><h2><strong>3. Speed degradation: latency compounds across the loop</strong></h2><p>A governed context layer can still fail if it's slow, because in agentic systems latency behaves like a correctness property. Slow retrieval changes what the agent does, not only how quickly it does it. The mechanism is multiplication: an agent doesn't retrieve once, it retrieves at every step of its reasoning loop. Exact counts vary by workload, but one analysis found that tool-augmented agents made <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://arxiv.org/html/2506.04301v1\">9.2 times more</a> LLM calls than plain chain-of-thought pipelines, and in one benchmark tool execution alone consumed <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://arxiv.org/html/2603.18897v1\">35% to 61%</a> of total request time. On numbers like those, retrieval dominates the clock, so a call that looks acceptable in isolation becomes seconds of accumulated wait, taking cost, throughput, and user experience with it.</p><h3><strong>Tail latency &amp; retry ripples</strong></h3><p>Tail latency makes the math worse. In the canonical example, a server is slow on just 1% of requests. Once a request fans out across 100 servers in parallel, that small tail gets amplified. At that fanout, it will slow <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://cacm.acm.org/research/the-tail-at-scale\">63% of user requests</a>.</p><p>Speed degradation also undermines reliability. Retrieval calls that hover near timeout thresholds create intermittent failures that are hard to reproduce and look like different bugs depending on where in the loop they occur. Retries then pile on: if they aren't randomly distributed, a small network blip can cause retry ripples that amplify themselves. The damage rarely gets attributed back to the context layer, which is what makes this failure mode so hard to fix.</p><h3><strong>The fix: fast context</strong></h3><p>Redis approaches this from two directions, both in service of fast context. Its memory-first architecture is designed to keep retrieval fast under load. In Redis' own vector database benchmarks, Redis reported <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://redis.io/blog/benchmarking-results-for-vector-databases/\">up to ~3.4x</a> higher queries per second (QPS) and up to ~4.7x lower latency than other vector databases tested at the same recall level. <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://redis.io/blog/context-window-management-llm-apps-developer-guide/\">Semantic caching</a> can shrink the call count in workloads with repeated intent: it stores LLM responses keyed by meaning, so a semantically similar prompt can return the cached answer instead of triggering a fresh inference call. <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://redis.io/langcache/\">Redis LangCache</a> is the fully managed semantic caching service in Iris. On cache hits, Redis reports up to 15x faster responses and <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://redis.io/blog/llm-token-optimization-speed-up-apps/\">up to 73%</a> lower LLM inference costs in high-repetition workloads.</p><h2><strong>4. Non-accumulation: agents that don't get better</strong></h2><p>The first three failure modes make an agent worse than it should be today. Non-accumulation is different in character: it caps how good the agent can ever get. Its value stays bounded by the quality of the static retrieval index and the model's generic reasoning, no matter how long the system has run or how many interactions it has processed, so the infrastructure depreciates rather than appreciates. A compounding system works the other way. Each interaction adds memory and personalization signal that makes the next interaction more useful.</p><h3><strong>What memory changes</strong></h3><p>The gap is measurable. In one benchmark session of 240 messages, an agent with structured memory management retained <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://arxiv.org/html/2603.13110v1\">99.6% of key information</a>, against 54.3% without memory management. In another study, memory-augmented personalization measured a <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://arxiv.org/html/2602.13258v1\">14.6% improvement</a> over a stateless baseline, with trait incorporation rising from 45% to 75%.</p><p>You can also watch what the absence of memory does to behavior. When a bug caused Claude Code's thinking history to be cleared each turn, the agent <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/april-23-postmortem\">kept executing without memory</a> of why it had chosen its earlier actions, which surfaced as forgetfulness, repetition, and odd tool choices.</p><h3><strong>The fix: compounding context</strong></h3><p>Agent memory is the piece of Iris built for compounding context. <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://redis.io/docs/latest/develop/ai/context-engine/agent-memory/\">Redis Agent Memory</a> uses a two-tier model: session memory holds active conversation state with a configurable time-to-live (TTL), while long-term memory stores extracted facts as text plus vector embeddings for semantic retrieval, and conversation events get promoted between tiers automatically and asynchronously, so extraction doesn't slow the interaction down. <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://redis.io/blog/ai-agent-orchestration-platforms/\">Agent frameworks</a> already wire into it: <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://docs.langchain.com/oss/python/langgraph/add-memory\">LangGraph ships a RedisStore</a>, and teams using Google's Agent Development Kit can route hot memory and vector retrieval through Redis for tighter latency control.</p><p>Memory does expand the attack surface, though. In one study, personalization increased attack success rates relative to stateless baselines across memory-augmented frameworks. The increase ranged from <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://arxiv.org/html/2601.17887v1\">15.8% to 243.7%</a>, which is one more reason the compounding layer needs the same access controls as the rest of the stack.</p><div><p><img alt=\"Search\" src=\"https://cdn.sanity.io/images/sy1jschh/production/aa307e0dd69103a86569000deb7f7d4a237d7ded-64x64.svg\"></p><div><h2>Your agents aren't the problem</h2><p><span>The data feeding them is. Grade your context layer and get a roadmap for fixing it.</span></p></div></div><h2><strong>Context that's navigable, fast, fresh, &amp; compounding</strong></h2><p>The four failure modes share a root cause: context gets treated as something you bolt onto an agent instead of infrastructure you design. Together they decide whether an agent can see reality, act on it in time, and get better at it. Choosing a context layer that supports all four pillars is the work, and it's cheaper before the first downstream incident than after.</p><p>That's the layer Redis Iris is built to be.
