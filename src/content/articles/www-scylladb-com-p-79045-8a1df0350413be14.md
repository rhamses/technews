Instead, take our words as a complementary reference. We also don’t claim to be experts in the art of Cassandra performance tuning or troubleshooting; rather, we’re practitioners who learned some things (the hard way).</p>\n<h2>Cassandra-Specific Tuning</h2>\n<p>At a minimum, focus your efforts on the following files:</p>\n<ul>\n<li><em><code>cassandra.yaml</code></em></li>\n<li><em><code>jvm[NN]-server.options</code></em></li>\n<li><em><code>jvm-server.options</code></em></li>\n</ul>\n<h3>cassandra.yaml</h3>\n<p>To help users get started, a stock Apache Cassandra installation ships with <strong>two</strong> config files. The first file – <em><code>cassandra.yaml</code></em> – is oriented for users upgrading from a previous Cassandra release and comes with <em>backward-compatible</em> settings. The second – <em><code>cassandra_latest.yaml</code></em> – “<em>contains configuration defaults that enable the latest features of Cassandra, including improved functionality as well as higher performance. This version is provided for new users of Cassandra who want to get the most out of their cluster, and for users evaluating the technology.</em>” Source: <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://cassandra.apache.org/doc/latest/cassandra/managing/configuration/cass_yaml_file.html\">the Cassandra project</a>.</p>\n<p>If you spin a fresh <em><code>cassandra:5</code></em> container or simply initiate your tuning journey without taking this into consideration, you’ll end up running your deployment under compatibility mode. The following command demonstrates how a freshly spun Cassandra 5 container starts under compatibility mode, rather than enabling its latest features:</p>\n<pre><code>root@container:/etc/cassandra# diff cassandra.yaml cassandra_latest.yaml | sed 's/^&gt;/[cassandra_latest.yaml]/g;s/^&lt;/[cassandra.yaml]/g' | egrep 'compatibility|memtable' | sort\r\n[cassandra.yaml] memtable_allocation_type: heap_buffers\r\n[cassandra.yaml] storage_compatibility_mode: CASSANDRA_4\r\n[cassandra_latest.yaml] memtable_allocation_type: offheap_objects\r\n[cassandra_latest.yaml] storage_compatibility_mode: NONE\r\n</code></pre>\n<p>It’s beyond the scope of this write-up to provide an exhaustive list of settings you should pay attention to when setting up Cassandra. The stock <strong>cassandra.yaml</strong> is often irrelevant, and we ended up simply replacing it with the <strong>cassandra\\_latest.yaml</strong> instead. If you are starting a fresh new cluster, we highly recommend you do the same.</p>\n<p>However, you probably  need to be extra cautious if you are an existing Cassandra user. Oftentimes the semantics of a particular setting may change entirely, making it particularly hard to track down. In one of our streaming performance tests, we noticed Cassandra’s streaming operations had a default cap of 24MiB/s per node, resulting in suboptimal transfer times. Upon raising those thresholds, we observed:</p>\n<ol>\n<li><a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://cassandra.apache.org/doc/4.0/cassandra/new/streaming.html\">Cassandra 4.0</a> docs mentioned tuning the <code>stream_throughput_outbound_megabits_per_sec</code> option</li>\n<li>Both <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://web.archive.org/web/20240829103756/https://cassandra.apache.org/doc/4.1/cassandra/architecture/streaming.html\">Cassandra 4.1</a> and <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://web.archive.org/web/20250213121633/https://cassandra.apache.org/doc/5.0/cassandra/architecture/streaming.html\">Cassandra 5.0</a> docs referenced the <code>stream_throughput_outbound</code> option</li>\n<li>Only reading <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://www.instaclustr.com/blog/cassandra_latest-yaml/\">this Instaclustr article</a> (or carefully interpreting <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://github.com/apache/cassandra/blob/trunk/conf/cassandra_latest.yaml\">cassandra\\_latest.yaml</a>) eventually shed some light on the correct option: <code>entire_sstable_stream_throughput_outbound</code>.</li>\n</ol>\n<p>In other words, 3 distinct settings exist for tuning the previous 3 major releases of Apache Cassandra – and one of them was incorrectly documented under the official project’s page. This raises concerns about the feasibility of upgrading from older releases. Given these constraints, we highly encourage organizations to conduct a careful review and full round of testing on their own. This is not an edge case; others noted similar upgrade problems on the <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://lists.apache.org/thread/otdq082ol1x1cylvgnj81or20532mwfb\">Apache Cassandra Mailing List</a>.</p>\n<p>With that in mind, here are some examples of misleading Cassandra config comments and why upgrades deserve some extra diligence:</p>\n<ul>\n<li><a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://issues.apache.org/jira/browse/CASSANDRA-16315\">CASSANDRA-16315</a> – Covers the <code>concurrent_compactors</code> setting</li>\n<li><a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://issues.apache.org/jira/browse/CASSANDRA-7139\">CASSANDRA-7139</a> – Describes how that same <code>concurrent_compactors</code> setting default was production unsafe when introduced</li>\n<li><a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://issues.apache.org/jira/browse/CASSANDRA-20692\">CASSANDRA-20692</a> – Describes how a commitlog correctness issue slipped through to Cassandra 5</li>\n</ul>\n<h3>JVM settings</h3>\n<table>\n<thead>\n<tr>\n<th>Test Kind</th>\n<th>Garbage Collector</th>\n<th>Read-ahead</th>\n<th>Compaction Throughput</th>\n<th>P99 Latency</th>\n<th>Throughput</th>\n</tr>\n</thead>\n<tbody>\n<tr>\n<td>Cassandra RA4 Compaction256</td>\n<td>ZGC</td>\n<td>4KB</td>\n<td>256MB/s</td>\n<td>6.662ms</td>\n<td>120K/s</td>\n</tr>\n<tr>\n<td>Cassandra RA4 Compaction0</td>\n<td>ZGC</td>\n<td>4KB</td>\n<td>Unthrottled</td>\n<td>8.159ms</td>\n<td>120K/s</td>\n</tr>\n<tr>\n<td>Cassandra RA8 Compaction256</td>\n<td>ZGC</td>\n<td>8KB</td>\n<td>256MB/s</td>\n<td>4.657ms</td>\n<td>100K/s</td>\n</tr>\n<tr>\n<td>Cassandra RA8 Compaction0</td>\n<td>ZGC</td>\n<td>8KB</td>\n<td>Unthrottled</td>\n<td>4.903ms</td>\n<td>100K/s</td>\n</tr>\n<tr>\n<td>Cassandra G1GC</td>\n<td>G1GC</td>\n<td>4KB</td>\n<td>256MB/s</td>\n<td>5.521ms</td>\n<td>40K/s</td>\n</tr>\n</tbody>\n</table>\n<p>Tuning the JVM is the least fun part of operating a Cassandra cluster. It can be a journey on its own, really. The good news is that Cassandra 5 includes support for JDK17, and users may now opt-in for using <code>ZGC</code> rather than the decades-long <code>G1</code> garbage collector.</p>\n<p>Unless you are a Java expert and know exactly what you are doing, this <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://thelastpickle.com/blog/2020/06/29/cassandra_4-0_garbage_collectors_performance_benchmarks.html\">theLastPickle</a> article is perhaps your best resource for tuning Cassandra’s JVM. You could read that and call it a day. Still, here are some details on what we’ve discovered along the way, since the DataStax (now IBM) <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://docs.datastax.com/en/cassandra-oss/3.0/cassandra/operations/opsTuneJVM.html\">Tuning Java resources</a> page only <em>advises</em> under a remark of adjusting “<em>settings gradually and test each incremental change”</em>:</p>\n<ol>\n<li>We’ve consistently measured lower latencies and higher throughput using <code>ZGC</code> under a handful of different scenarios. Although we’ve seen some users reporting good <code>G1</code> performance results, this doesn’t align with what we’ve experimented with in practice.</li>\n<li>Remember that Cassandra relies on both off-heap as well as on-heap memory. The heap size will depend on how much RAM your setup has. Since we primarily test on 128GB RAM machines, we found that allocating beyond 32G would be wasteful.</li>\n<li><a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://thelastpickle.com/blog/2020/06/29/cassandra_4-0_garbage_collectors_performance_benchmarks.html\">theLastPickle</a>‘s article mentioned earlier makes a good point about compressed OOPs, though we believe this should be relevant for RAM constrained systems. We didn’t observe any noticeable benefits/disadvantages from having <code>31G/32G</code> in our results.</li>\n<li>Most of the JVM settings will sit under the <em><code>jvm17-server.options</code></em> file (if you’re using JDK17). However, there is yet another file (<em><code>jvm-server.options</code></em>, note there’s no Java version) that you should also edit. Apparently Cassandra has some built-in scriptology in <em><code>cassandra.in.sh</code></em> that looks up the latter and inherits options from it. Then, if your heap settings (<code>-Xmx</code> &amp; <code>-Xms</code>) are unset, it will automatically define it for you:</li>\n</ol>\n<pre><code>#################\r\n# HEAP SETTINGS #\r\n#################\n# Heap size is automatically calculated by cassandra-env based on this\r\n# formula: max(min(1/2 ram, 1024MB), min(1/4 ram, 8GB))\r\n# That is:\r\n# - calculate 1/2 ram and cap to 1024MB\r\n# - calculate 1/4 ram and cap to 8192MB\r\n# - pick the max\r\n#\r\n# For production use you may wish to adjust this for your environment.\r\n# If that's the case, uncomment the -Xmx and Xms options below to override the\r\n# automatic calculation of JVM heap memory.\r\n#\r\n# It is recommended to set min (-Xms) and max (-Xmx) heap sizes to\r\n# the same value to avoid stop-the-world GC pauses during resize, and\r\n# so that we can lock the heap in memory on startup to prevent any\r\n# of it from being swapped out.\r\n#-Xms4G\r\n#-Xmx4G\r\n</code></pre>\n<p>Therefore, uncomment and override the two lines above for your environment.</p>\n<ol>\n<li>After you are done, you may want to circle back to the <em><code>cassandra.yaml</code></em> file because there are some settings that influence your heap allocation. For example:\n<ul>\n<li><code>networking_cache_size</code></li>\n<li><code>file_cache_size</code></li>\n<li><code>memtable_offheap_space</code></li>\n<li><code>repair_session_space</code></li>\n<li>among others…</li>\n</ul>\n<p>If you feel like Cassandra is choking and the system is not under heap pressure, then playing with these settings is probably your next step. Sadly, this is where things become trial-and-error, and even more time consuming. (Though, in Cassandra’s defense, tuning most of these parameters is <em>workload specific</em>).</p></li>\n</ol>\n<h3>About Cassandra Caching</h3>\n<p>Apache Cassandra ships two caching-related settings:</p>\n<ul>\n<li><code>row_cache_size</code> and</li>\n<li><code>key_cache_size</code></li>\n</ul>\n<p>You should almost never enable either of these settings (<code>0GiB</code> means these are disabled). Consider using the operating system page cache instead of the row cache, because writes to a partition invalidate the whole partition in the cache.</em></p>\n<h2>OS Tuning</h2>\n<p>Operating system tuning for Cassandra shares many similarities with other databases. Preventing swapping, tuning the kernel via <code>sysctl</code>, setting disk <code>read_ahead_kb</code> settings, configuring user limits and enabling <code>Transparent HugePages</code> are the primary settings we touch when deploying Cassandra. This is (undoubtedly) a non-exhaustive list, although it should cover the strategies seen across most production Cassandra deployments in practice.</p>\n<p>Depending on your setup, you may want to further check:</p>\n<ul>\n<li>your <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://www.brendangregg.com/blog/2021-09-26/the-speed-of-time.html\">clocksource</a> – particularly under Xen hypervisors;</li>\n<li>whether <code>cpupower</code> supports setting the CPU scaling governor to “<code>performance</code>” mode;</li>\n<li>experimenting with <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://databasetorque.com/jemalloc-and-cassandra/\">jemalloc</a>;</li>\n<li>configuring <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://docs.kernel.org/core-api/irq/irq-affinity.html\">SMP IRQ Affinity</a>;</li>\n<li>and pinning Cassandra to specific CPUs via <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://man7.org/linux/man-pages/man1/taskset.1.html\">taskset(1)</a>.</li>\n</ul>\n<h3>Disks</h3>\n<p>We primarily store Cassandra related files (including its related logs) on locally-attached NVMe disks, as commonly found within cloud hyperscalers. If there’s more than one attached disk to the VM, we combine them into a <code>RAID-0</code> array using <code>mdadm</code>. In addition, we use <code>XFS</code> as the backing filesystem, particularly as it’s the same we use for ScyllaDB.</p>\n<p>We also set only one-hit merges, limit <code>read_ahead_kb</code> to just <code>4kB</code>, and disable the IO scheduler (if any):</p>\n<pre><code>MD_NAME=nvme1n1\nsudo sh -c \"echo 1 &gt; /sys/block/$MD_NAME/queue/nomerges\"\r\nsudo sh -c \"echo 4 &gt; /sys/block/$MD_NAME/queue/read_ahead_kb\"\r\nsudo sh -c \"echo none &gt; /sys/block/$MD_NAME/queue/scheduler\"\r\n</code></pre>\n<p>Some important remarks:</p>\n<ul>\n<li>the <code>scheduler</code> command may “fail” in modern Cloud instances (and that’s fine);</li>\n<li>when using <code>mdadm</code>, tune each block device individually backing the RAID device;</li>\n<li><code>read_ahead_kb</code> is a workload dependent setting. We often test small partition lookups, but workloads with larger wide-rows may benefit from increasing that setting.</li>\n</ul>\n<h3>Memory</h3>\n<p>We don’t configure swapping at all to keep matters simple. The rationale is that Cassandra already benefits from the OS page cache, and we leave over half of the server’s RAM just for it.</p>\n<p>During our tests, we also observed that enabling <code>Transparent Huge pages</code>, especially with <code>ZGC</code>, contributed positively to Cassandra’s performance. Although the improvement wasn’t remarkable, we observed positive results similar to what both <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://tobert.github.io/tldr/cassandra-java-huge-pages.html#:~:text=A%20Linux%20feature%20called%20%22Transparent%20Hugepages%22.%20When,typically%202MB%20%2D%20rather%20than%204K%20at\">Amy</a> and <a target=\"_blank\" href=\"https://netflixtechblog.com/bending-pause-times-to-your-will-with-generational-zgc-256629c9386b\">Netflix</a> reported.</p>\n<p>The provided links already go in-depth on how to enable <code>THP</code>, as well as how to configure Cassandra to benefit from it. Keep in mind, however, that we recommend you set the <code>-XX:+AlwaysPreTouch</code> JVM option regardless of whether <code>THP</code> is enabled or not. That’s because it’s known to improve overall JVM runtime performance at the expense of increased JVM startup times.</p>\n<h3>Kernel and User limits</h3>\n<p>Put simply, you don’t want Cassandra to be limited on either networking, memory allocation, or the number of files it can open. We set <code>sysctl.conf.d/99-cassandra.conf</code> to the following values:</p>\n<pre><code>net.ipv4.tcp_keepalive_time=60\r\nnet.ipv4.tcp_keepalive_probes=3\r\nnet.ipv4.tcp_keepalive_intvl=10\r\nnet.core.rmem_default=16777216\r\nnet.core.wmem_default=16777216\r\nnet.core.optmem_max=40960\r\nvm.max_map_count = 1048575\r\nnet.ipv4.tcp_rmem = 4096 87380 16777216\r\nnet.ipv4.tcp_wmem = 4096 65536 16777216\r\nnet.core.rmem_max = 16777216\r\nnet.core.wmem_max = 16777216\r\nnet.core.netdev_max_backlog = 2500\r\nnet.core.somaxconn = 65000\r\nnet.ipv4.tcp_ecn = 0\r\nnet.ipv4.tcp_window_scaling = 1\r\nnet.ipv4.ip_local_port_range = 10000 65535\r\nnet.ipv4.tcp_syncookies = 0\r\nnet.ipv4.tcp_timestamps = 0\r\nnet.ipv4.tcp_sack = 0\r\nnet.ipv4.tcp_fack = 1\r\nnet.ipv4.tcp_dsack = 1\r\nnet.ipv4.tcp_orphan_retries = 1\r\nvm.dirty_background_bytes = 10485760\r\nvm.dirty_bytes = 1073741824\r\nvm.zone_reclaim_mode = 0\r\nfs.file-max = 1073741824\r\nvm.max_map_count = 1073741824\r\n</code></pre>\n<p>Lastly, the user running Cassandra must be allowed to allocate enough resources for the process to run. As our VMs are short-lived, we enable unlimited <code>limits.conf</code> consumption to all users:</p>\n<pre><code>* - nofile     1000000\r\n* - memlock    unlimited\r\n* - fsize      unlimited\r\n* - data       unlimited\r\n* - rss        unlimited\r\n* - stack      unlimited\r\n* - cpu        unlimited\r\n* - nproc      unlimited\r\n* - as         unlimited\r\n* - locks      unlimited\r\n* - sigpending unlimited\r\n* - msgqueue   unlimited\r\n</code></pre>\n<h2>Parting Thoughts</h2>\n<p>As demonstrated, Apache Cassandra performance tuning is far from a one-size-fits-all solution. The settings described throughout this article represent what worked for our specific hardware setups and workload profiles. If your deployment spans different hardware, many of the values presented here will likely need to be revisited.</p>\n<p>This brings us to (perhaps) the most underappreciated cost in Cassandra operations: dependency. That is, every tuning decision is implicitly a contract with the underlying hardware. Adding more disks, increasing CPU/RAM, changing workloads are some overlooked aspects that will require entirely new tuning cycles and re-evaluating your previous decisions.</p>\n<p>ScyllaDB was designed with this problem in mind.
